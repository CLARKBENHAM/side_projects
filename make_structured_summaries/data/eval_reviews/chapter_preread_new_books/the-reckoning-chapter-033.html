Orientation Before Reading

  • Early 1970s Ford is still organized around big-car profits, but the market is starting to move: Volkswagen has made small cars respectable, Japanese cars are gaining in California, and Europe is proving that small cars can be desirable rather than merely cheap.
  • The technical question is front-wheel drive: not just a different layout, but a whole different way to build smaller, lighter, more fuel-efficient cars.
  • The business question is whether Ford can afford to spend heavily on a low-margin small car when its wages, executive expectations, dealer economics, and profit habits are built around large vehicles.
  • The political question is inseparable from the product question. Henry Ford II and Lee Iacocca are already locked in a personal struggle, so every product decision becomes a test of power and loyalty.
  • Hal Sperlich, Iacocca’s favored product man, becomes the carrier of the small-car argument. Philip Caldwell, head of Ford of Europe and aligned more with systems and finance discipline, becomes an obstacle or at least a source of drag.
  • The oil shock after the 1973 Yom Kippur War turns a strategic question into a crisis. Ford suddenly needs fuel-efficient cars, but the company is emotionally, technically, and financially unprepared.

The Chapter Arc

  • Ford begins by misreading early warnings from California, Volkswagen, and Japanese imports; what looks like a fringe market is actually the first sign that affluent and younger buyers may want different cars.
  • Sperlich’s European assignment changes the small-car question from theory into product conviction: after driving European front-wheel-drive cars, he sees that small cars can be efficient, nimble, and pleasurable.
  • The old Detroit assumption that Americans dislike small cars weakens; Sperlich reframes the issue as Detroit having built bad small cars, then using their failure as proof that small cars could not sell.
  • The Fiesta project moves from idea to corporate battle. Iacocca supports it, finance resists it, Caldwell slows or complicates it, and Henry Ford hesitates over cost and profit.
  • The Fiesta survives because of external factors as much as product merit: Henry Ford wants a Spanish manufacturing foothold, and currency shifts make Volkswagen’s competing Rabbit more expensive.
  • The Ford-Iacocca relationship deteriorates after the oil shock, especially when Henry Ford pulls $2 billion from new-product development. Iacocca sees retreat; Ford sees caution and survival.
  • Public scenes begin to expose private fracture: Henry Ford’s drunken remarks to analysts and later outburst in Germany make the leadership split visible inside and outside the company.
  • Ford’s legal audit of Iacocca and Bill Fugazy changes the internal balance. Iacocca remains formally powerful, but everyone now knows the chairman is looking for a way to weaken or remove him.
  • By the mid-1970s, Ford must decide how to downsize its American line under federal mileage rules. The same fight returns at greater scale: Sperlich and Iacocca want a state-of-the-art small car; Henry Ford and finance fear a ruinously expensive bet.

What To Watch For

  • Ford’s wage and profit structure matters: big salaries and union costs push the company toward big cars because big cars carry the margins that sustain the system.
  • Front-wheel drive is presented as a compounding engineering advantage: less weight allows a smaller engine, which reduces weight again, making the whole car more efficient.
  • Halberstam keeps contrasting product feel with financial control. Sperlich and Iacocca judge cars by possibility and market movement; Caldwell and finance judge them by risk, cost, and projected return.
  • The Fiesta decision is not a clean triumph of foresight. It passes because product logic, Spanish labor strategy, and German currency movements happen to align.
  • Henry Ford’s opposition is not merely technical or financial. His age, health, family-company anxieties, and suspicion of Iacocca all shape product strategy.
  • The oil shock reveals a timing trap: Detroit must redesign cars just as inflation rises, capital becomes expensive, emissions work consumes engineering capacity, and consumers remain uncertain.
  • Federal mileage standards force companies to sell a fleet mix, not just individual profitable cars. A good small car can make it legally possible to keep selling profitable large cars.
  • Finance’s weakness, in Halberstam’s telling, is not arithmetic but imagination: its projections do not account for energy-price instability or a changed market.
  • Loyalty is a central operating constraint at Ford. The question is often not “Is this the right car?” but “Whose car is this, and what does supporting it imply?”

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Sperlich drives the Fiat 127.**

Setup: Iacocca sends Sperlich to Europe to study the lower end of the market. Action: Sperlich drives a small Fiat and is struck by its handling and pleasure. Payoff: front-wheel drive stops being an eccentric engineering idea and becomes, for him, the future Ford must meet.

**Fred Hooven’s reverse memo.**

Setup: Ford has long dismissed front-wheel-drive advocates as oddballs. Action: Hooven writes as if rear-wheel drive were the strange new proposal, listing its disadvantages as benefits. Payoff: the joke exposes how much of Detroit’s logic depends on inherited assumptions rather than engineering merit.

**The Fiesta approval.**

Setup: Sperlich’s small European car tests well but faces cost objections and internal resistance. Action: the project survives because Henry Ford wants a Spanish plant and because currency changes improve the business case against Volkswagen. Payoff: Ford gets one of its best small cars, but the narrowness of the decision shows how fragile product innovation is inside the company.

**The analyst dinner in New York.**

Setup: Ford executives are trying to reassure Wall Street after the oil shock. Action: Henry Ford drinks heavily and tells analysts the company is headed nowhere; Iacocca has to salvage the evening. Payoff: Ford cancels the rest of the tour and becomes more suspicious of Iacocca’s public visibility.

**The German dinner outburst.**

Setup: Iacocca’s group sits together during a Ford of Germany event, sharpening Henry Ford’s fear of a separate Iacocca faction. Action: Ford angrily denounces Americans who do not socialize with the Germans. Payoff: the top-level split becomes publicly visible, and Ford’s loyalty question moves into the open.

**The Fugazy audit.**

Setup: Iacocca’s friendship with New York fixer Bill Fugazy troubles Henry Ford. Action: Ford launches an expensive legal audit that finds no cause to fire Iacocca. Payoff: even without proving wrongdoing, the audit damages Iacocca by signaling that the chairman is now his enemy.

Cast And Terms

  • **Henry Ford II**: Ford chairman; increasingly cautious, ill, protective of family control, and hostile to Iacocca.
  • **Lee Iacocca**: Ford president; brilliant product and marketing executive, ambitious, combative, and increasingly isolated.
  • **Hal Sperlich**: Iacocca’s favored product planner; chief advocate for front-wheel-drive small cars.
  • **Philip Caldwell**: Head of Ford of Europe; careful, system-oriented executive seen by product men as slow and risk-averse.
  • **Ed Lundy**: Powerful finance executive; part of the anti-Iacocca/finance camp.
  • **Fiesta**: Ford’s small front-wheel-drive European car, developed under Sperlich’s push.
  • **Rabbit/Golf**: Volkswagen’s new small front-wheel-drive competitor.
  • **Front-wheel drive**: Layout putting engine, power train, and drive wheels in front, allowing lighter and more space-efficient cars.
  • **Fleet average**: Government fuel-economy rule based on the average mileage of all cars a company sells.
  • **Bill Fugazy**: New York travel-business figure and Iacocca friend whose Ford dealings become a loyalty issue.

What To Hold Lightly

  • Caldwell’s grape juice, bottled water, antiques, and anti-smoking habits are vivid character evidence, but the main point is his decision style.
  • The 727 airplane and Cristina Iacocca’s travels matter mostly as symbols of privilege, resentment, and Henry Ford’s growing irritation.
  • The Christopher Columbus Club dinner is less about the event itself than about Iacocca’s hunger for recognition beyond Detroit.
  • The Edsel comparison is useful scale-setting: old mistakes were embarrassing; mid-1970s mistakes could threaten the company.
