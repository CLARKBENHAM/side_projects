## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in early-1960s Ford, just after Robert McNamara leaves for Washington and Lee Iacocca takes over the Ford division at age thirty-six.
- The larger setting is postwar American abundance: suburbs are expanding, gas is cheap, television is becoming a selling machine, and families are buying second and third cars.
- The central conflict is between product-and-sales people, represented by Iacocca and Don Frey, and finance people, represented by Ed Lundy’s faction.
- Iacocca’s power comes from dealers, market instinct, preparation, and personal ambition. He knows how cars are actually sold, especially on monthly payments and options.
- Halberstam frames Iacocca as both Ford insider and ethnic outsider: the son of Italian immigrants, driven by wariness, resentment, and the need to prove himself.
- The Mustang becomes the chapter’s proving ground: a youth car that tests whether Ford can still follow market instinct instead of merely defending standard volume.
- The success of the Mustang is also dangerous because Iacocca claims public credit in a company where only Henry Ford II is supposed to own success.

## The Chapter Arc

- Iacocca rises with the postwar market, shifting Ford’s dealer map from old downtowns toward the new suburbs where car-dependent affluence is forming.
- McNamara’s departure changes Iacocca’s position from talented subordinate to liberated divisional chief, free to reverse the utilitarian, cautious bias he disliked.
- Iacocca’s rivalry with finance hardens: he learns to defer outwardly to Ed Lundy while privately treating finance as a force that misunderstands cars, dealers, and customers.
- Halberstam widens the frame from corporate politics to biography, showing Iacocca’s ambition as rooted in immigrant family history and distrust of the Protestant corporate order.
- Charley Beacham becomes the formative sales mentor, teaching Iacocca that cars are pushed through dealers, salesmen, incentives, urgency, and cash flow.
- Iacocca’s early “56 for ’56” campaign turns him from promising salesman into a Ford star, proving he can move weak product by understanding buyer psychology.
- After McNamara, Iacocca begins dismantling the old regime: he attacks the Falcon’s austerity, kills the Cardinal, restores racing, and re-centers selling over safety or economy.
- The Mustang develops through a partnership and rivalry with Don Frey: Frey supplies much of the product imagination, while Iacocca supplies political force and market framing.
- The Mustang’s launch transforms Iacocca into a national figure, but his public identification with the car starts the slow erosion of his relationship with Henry Ford II.

## What To Watch For

- Watch how Halberstam distinguishes sticker price from monthly payment. Iacocca’s edge is that he thinks like the buyer and dealer, not like an executive buying for cash.
- The finance concept of “standard volume” matters: it treats last year’s sales as protected profit and makes new product seem like a threat before it is an opportunity.
- The Mustang is not expensive innovation in the usual sense. Its risk is political and perceptual more than technical, because it reuses Falcon components.
- Iacocca repeatedly works outside formal channels. He knows committees favor finance, so he tries to make Henry Ford emotionally attached to a car before the numbers people freeze it.
- The chapter treats dealers as Ford’s real first customers. Ford sells cars to dealers, and dealers sell them onward; Iacocca’s loyalty base is built around that reality.
- Frey and Iacocca are complementary but not identical. Frey loves machinery and product; Iacocca loves selling, power, and what the car can do for his career.
- Halberstam admires Iacocca’s market instinct but keeps returning to ego, anger, and personalization as future liabilities.
- The Mustang’s huge success should not be read only as product triumph. Halberstam says the near failure to approve it reveals how strong the finance faction had become.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Iacocca and Lundy over monthly cost**  
Setup: Lundy knows sticker prices; Iacocca knows ordinary buyers finance cars. Action: Iacocca instantly names the monthly installment and sees Lundy’s confusion. Payoff: the scene crystallizes the split between abstract financial control and street-level selling.

**Beacham sends Iacocca to move used trucks**  
Setup: A dealer is stuck with forty unsold used trucks in Pottstown. Action: Iacocca discounts the “used iron,” clears the lot, and reports victory. Payoff: Beacham simply tells him to move on to the next town, teaching that selling is continuous pressure, not a congratulatory event.

**The killing of the Cardinal**  
Setup: McNamara has committed Ford to a small, efficient German-designed car after $35 million has already been spent. Action: Iacocca sees it, hates it, and tells Henry Ford it will recall the Edsel. Payoff: the car dies, and Iacocca gains standing by decisively undoing McNamara’s legacy.

**Henry Ford rejects the Mustang pitch**  
Setup: Iacocca brings Henry to the design center expecting the car to sell itself. Action: Henry abruptly says he does not want to talk about it and leaves. Payoff: the rejection looks fatal until illness explains part of it, and Iacocca shifts to surrounding Henry with outside excitement.

**The production gamble**  
Setup: Market research suggests tens of thousands, perhaps 100,000 sales. Action: Iacocca prepares first one plant, then two, then three. Payoff: Ford can meet demand when the Mustang becomes a phenomenon, turning instinct into profit.

**The magazine covers**  
Setup: Iacocca wants public ownership of the Mustang story. Action: he works Time and Newsweek and lands both covers in the same week. Payoff: his fame spikes, but Ford insiders see that he has crossed an unwritten line.

## Cast And Terms

- **Lee Iacocca**: Ford division chief, salesman-politician, future public face of the Mustang.
- **Henry Ford II**: Ford’s powerful, mercurial head; approval is necessary, and personal credit belongs to him by company custom.
- **Robert McNamara**: Former Ford president, then Secretary of Defense; associated here with finance discipline, safety, economy, and the Falcon.
- **Ed Lundy**: Senior finance executive and Henry Ford’s trusted support; represents the growing power of financial control.
- **Don Frey**: Product manager and engineer; central technical and conceptual force behind the Mustang.
- **Charley Beacham**: Eastern district sales manager; Iacocca’s rough sales mentor.
- **Falcon**: McNamara’s practical compact car; becomes both Mustang platform and Iacocca’s foil.
- **Cardinal**: Small German Ford project killed by Iacocca before U.S. launch.
- **Standard volume**: Finance baseline of sales Ford expects without extra spending.
- **Decrements**: Finance label for changes that may add cost or reduce protected sales.
- **Dealers**: Independent retail network; in Halberstam’s account, Ford’s immediate customers and Iacocca’s political base.

## What To Hold Lightly

- Beacham’s jokes, cigar habits, and rustic sayings are vivid, but their main purpose is to show the old sales culture that shaped Iacocca.
- The ethnic ditty about Giuseppe Tomassi matters as atmosphere of prejudice, not as a fact to retain in detail.
- Frey’s childhood camera and bicycle stories mainly establish his tinkerer’s temperament.
- The exact magazine-bureau maneuvering matters less than the consequence: Iacocca publicly attached himself to the Mustang.
- Some sales numbers are important, but the larger contrast is simpler: finance projected caution; Iacocca prepared for volume; demand exploded.
