## Orientation Before Reading

- Nissan is entering the high-growth years around 1958-1961: Japan’s domestic car market is beginning to open, and the company has a rare chance to become a mass producer rather than a struggling manufacturer.
- The central tension is capacity. Nissan’s engineers and product people believe demand is taking off and want to build big; president Katsuji Kawamata sees the same opportunity but is frightened by the debt required to seize it.
- Toyota is the measuring stick and the threat. Its Motomachi plant gives Nissan’s people a concrete sign that Toyota is preparing earlier and more boldly for the coming consumer market.
- The chapter moves between factory-floor modernization and executive politics. Nissan is becoming technically stronger, but its decision-making structure is cautious, factional, and personally controlled.
- Kinichi Tamura represents the exhilarated production engineers building the “new Nissan” through machines, plant expansion, and improvised learning from Detroit.
- Kawamata represents financial caution and political command: he limits capital risk, consolidates power through the union leadership, and later claims an industrial stature many colleagues think he has not earned.
- Masaru Miyake and Ichiro Shioji, formally union leaders, function as power brokers inside management. Their support protects Kawamata and gives them veto power over personnel and internal advancement.

## The Chapter Arc

- Nissan begins the 1960s under severe strain but with its labor crisis behind it; the company is no longer merely surviving, and its borrowed money is being converted into machines, factories, and industrial capability.
- Tamura’s factory world changes from routine production into chaotic construction: old lines are torn apart while output must keep rising, making modernization feel both exhilarating and barely controllable.
- Nissan’s engineers move from dependence on American machinery toward self-confidence when they design their first transfer machine from sketches, photos, and lessons brought back by productivity teams.
- The successful installation of that transfer machine marks a psychological break inside the company: Nissan’s engineers see themselves as builders of a modern industrial system, not just imitators catching up.
- The story shifts to Kawamata’s dilemma: as the domestic market explodes, his caution turns from prudence into a bottleneck, because every month of delay gives Toyota more room.
- The Oppama plant opportunity changes the stakes from abstract expansion to a specific site and deadline, but Kawamata’s hesitation costs Nissan time even after the land appears unusually well suited.
- Shiro Matsuzaki’s push for a 20,000-car-per-month factory turns the capacity debate into a direct confrontation between production ambition and financial fear.
- Kawamata’s compromise does not solve the problem; the new plant is capped at 5,000 cars a month, starts life overloaded, and forces Nissan toward double shifts and then another plant near Zama.
- The chapter closes by revealing that Kawamata’s power rests less on product judgment than on political survival, union alliance, and status performance, culminating in the living executive’s statue at Oppama.

## What To Watch For

- Watch the difference between technical modernization and strategic boldness. Nissan’s engineers can improvise, learn, and build; top management still struggles to commit at the scale the market requires.
- Toyota matters mostly as timing pressure. The chapter repeatedly measures Nissan’s delay against Toyota’s earlier plant planning, making lateness a competitive fact, not just an internal frustration.
- Debt is not background accounting here. It is the force that shapes Kawamata’s caution, limits plant size, and explains why even critics later hesitate between anger and gratitude.
- The transfer machine is both a practical tool and a symbol. It reduces labor, speeds production, and gives Nissan’s engineers proof that they can internalize Detroit methods in Japanese conditions.
- Pay attention to capacity percentages and monthly output targets. The argument over 5,000, 7,000, and 20,000 cars a month is the chapter’s concrete measure of ambition.
- The union’s role is inverted from a simple labor-versus-management story. Miyake and Shioji protect Kawamata, influence management appointments, and act as internal kingmakers.
- Halberstam is skeptical of public hierarchy and private resentment. Japanese corporate harmony appears as a surface discipline that contains, rather than eliminates, factional struggle.
- Kawamata’s status rituals matter because they reveal how power converts into narrative: he opposed or shrank major moves, yet later presents himself as the man who made Nissan great.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Tamura in the half-demolished factory**: Nissan is expanding while still trying to meet production schedules. Tamura watches newly installed equipment get knocked down by construction cranes and floor markings destroyed by work crews. The payoff is the lived sense of modernization as exhaustion, not clean progress.

**The homemade transfer machine**: Senior executives ask whether Nissan can build for itself a machine Detroit would simply buy. Tamura and his colleagues assemble a design from American trip notes, photographs, and sketches, then hand it to a local machine shop. The payoff is a leap in confidence: the impossible becomes manageable once broken into smaller pieces.

**The two-day celebration**: When the transfer machine arrives, work in the plant stops and a party breaks out. The action is less formal ceremony than collective recognition. The payoff is Tamura’s later memory of this as the dividing line between old Nissan and new Nissan.

**Kawamata and the Oppama delay**: Nissan discovers a promising site near Yokosuka, but Kawamata stalls for months while Toyota is already building. The action is procrastination under financial fear. The payoff is a permanent competitive handicap: Nissan starts the plant race about three years behind Toyota.

**Matsuzaki’s appeal for a huge plant**: A young engineer, inspired by Kawasaki Steel’s Yataro Nishiyama, asks Kawamata to approve a 20,000-car-per-month factory. Kawamata answers with the burden of begging bankers for money and the shame if the project fails. The payoff is a shrunken Oppama, overloaded from the start.

**The failed coup against Kawamata**: Asahara and others try to move Kawamata to Nissan Diesel, but Miyake intervenes through the Industrial Bank and a symbolic strike. The payoff is decisive: Kawamata survives, Asahara’s position weakens, and the union becomes essential to Kawamata’s rule.

## Cast And Terms

- **Nissan**: Japanese automaker moving from postwar instability into mass-production growth.
- **Katsuji Kawamata**: Nissan president, former Industrial Bank man; financially cautious, politically effective, dependent on union allies.
- **Kinichi Tamura**: Nissan design engineer who helps develop and install the company’s first transfer machine.
- **Transfer machine**: Long automated production machine combining many station tasks, central to Detroit-style mass production.
- **Toyota Motomachi plant**: Toyota’s new assembly plant, begun from an old airplane factory; a sign Toyota is moving faster into mass production.
- **Oppama**: Large plant site near Yokosuka, south of Yokohama; Nissan’s delayed and undersized answer to Toyota’s expansion.
- **Shiro Matsuzaki**: Young Nissan engineer who argues for a much larger Oppama plant.
- **Yataro Nishiyama**: Kawasaki Steel leader admired for bold expansion against conservative banking opposition.
- **Hisato Ichimada**: Conservative head of the Bank of Japan, nicknamed “the Pope.”
- **Masaru Miyake**: Nissan union leader whose support saves and strengthens Kawamata.
- **Ichiro Shioji**: Miyake’s ambitious deputy, part of the union-management power bloc.
- **Asahara**: Nissan president before Kawamata; leads the failed effort to remove him.
- **Kyoichi Harashina**: Technically respected Nissan executive whom many product people see as the natural successor, but whom the union blocks.
- **Industrial Bank of Japan / IBJ**: Powerful financial institution with influence over Nissan leadership.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The comic “Yes-No” and “No-Yes” description of Kawamata captures his indecision, but the more important point is the delayed capital commitment.
- The factory mishaps around cranes and erased floor markings are vivid texture; the deeper issue is modernization under continuous production pressure.
- The golf-course flattery of Kawamata is colorful, but it mainly reinforces the broader pattern of status management around him.
- The statue itself is memorable, but its significance lies in what it says about Kawamata’s self-image, Miyake’s political intelligence, and Nissan’s internal hierarchy.
