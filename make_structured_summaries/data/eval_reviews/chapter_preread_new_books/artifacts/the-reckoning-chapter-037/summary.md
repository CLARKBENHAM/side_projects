## 1. Orientation Before Reading

- Nissan has moved from postwar underdog to global threat. Takashi Ishihara inherits not the easy problem of making Nissan successful, but the harder one of staying successful while Western protectionism and racial resentment rise.
- The central external question is whether Nissan will build in the United States, and if so whether that plant will make cars or trucks, where it will be located, and whether it will accept the UAW.
- The central internal conflict is Ishihara versus Ichiro Shioji. Shioji, once nearly untouchable inside Nissan, is allied with the UAW and wants a unionized American auto plant; Ishihara wants control and distrusts American labor.
- Pat Greathouse, the UAW’s international affairs man, becomes the American trying to turn Japanese market success into American jobs. His problem is that Japanese politeness often sounds like commitment when it is not.
- Doug Fraser, UAW president, enters later and turns a quiet negotiation into open confrontation, exposing how much contempt and resentment sit beneath the formal U.S.-Japan alliance.
- The chapter is less about one factory decision than about a shift in power: Japanese companies no longer seek Western favor; American workers and politicians now seek concessions from Japan.

## 2. The Chapter Arc

- Ishihara’s presidency is framed as a new kind of burden: Nissan’s success has made every business decision political, especially in the United States.
- Halberstam establishes Ishihara as ambitious, isolated, financially skilled, and culturally wary of foreigners, which changes the reader’s view of Nissan’s overseas choices from purely strategic to deeply personal.
- Nissan’s possible American factory moves from vague goodwill to a UAW objective, with Greathouse believing early conversations amount to a commitment.
- Shioji warns Greathouse that the mood inside Nissan is changing, which turns the American plant from a likely concession into a contested internal power issue.
- Greathouse invents the “250,000-car rule” as a political standard: if a Japanese company sells more than that many cars annually in America, it should produce there too.
- Ishihara’s reluctance hardens as American protectionism rises; instead of making a U.S. plant more attractive, the hostile climate reinforces Japanese fears about race, unions, and being trapped abroad.
- The Ishihara-Shioji feud becomes the hidden machinery behind the negotiation: anything Shioji wants becomes harder for Ishihara to accept.
- Fraser’s 1980 Tokyo meeting with Ishihara breaks through diplomatic manners and becomes a direct argument over Japanese protectionism, American labor, and democratic legitimacy.
- Nissan finally chooses a southern U.S. truck plant, later announced for Tennessee, but the result is not the UAW victory Greathouse and Shioji had sought. The plant will be American but, if Nissan can manage it, nonunion.

## 3. What To Watch For

- Watch the distinction between promise and implication. American negotiators hear assurances; Japanese executives leave themselves room to maneuver.
- Ishihara’s view of America matters: he loves it as a market and distrusts it as a production site. That split drives the factory decision.
- Shioji’s American friendships have flipped in value. In earlier decades, U.S. ties strengthened him; now they make him vulnerable inside a more confident, nationalist Nissan.
- The UAW leadership remains more internationalist than its rank and file, but pressure from workers is rising as imports threaten jobs.
- Halberstam keeps tying labor politics to national character claims. Japanese executives criticize American workers; American union leaders answer by attacking Japanese hierarchy and lack of openness.
- The factory location is not a neutral detail. A northern plant would be more accessible to the UAW; a southern plant gives Nissan a better chance to avoid unionization.
- The truck-versus-car choice also matters. Shioji wants cars, the heart of the industry; Nissan’s truck plant is a narrower and more controlled concession.
- Toyota’s caution appears as confirmation that Nissan’s hesitation is not just Ishihara’s eccentricity. Japanese automakers fear committing capital just as American firms might recover.
- The England comparison at the end sharpens Fraser’s anger: Nissan’s willingness to deal with British unions undercuts its claim that union labor itself is impossible.

## 4. Load-Bearing Scenes

**Ishihara Inherits The Hard Years**  
Setup: Kawamata had led Nissan when Japan was poorer and less visible.  
Action: Ishihara takes power when Japanese success has made the company politically exposed abroad.  
Payoff: Nissan’s decisions now have to manage resentment, protectionism, and national pride, not just profit.

**Greathouse Hears A Commitment**  
Setup: The UAW wants Japanese companies selling heavily in America to create American jobs.  
Action: Greathouse meets Nissan executives, especially Masataka Okuma, and believes Nissan has effectively agreed to a U.S. plant and a union relationship.  
Payoff: The chapter later shows how costly that interpretation is; what sounded like agreement was closer to courteous ambiguity.

**Shioji Warns Greathouse**  
Setup: Shioji has long been the UAW’s friend inside Nissan and wants an American union plant.  
Action: He tells Greathouse that Nissan is pulling back and that his own leverage is weakening because of conflict with Ishihara.  
Payoff: The American negotiation is revealed as dependent on a Japanese power struggle Greathouse cannot control.

**The Fraser-Ishihara Blowup**  
Setup: Fraser arrives in Tokyo expecting hard but civil talks over exports, protectionism, and a U.S. factory.  
Action: Ishihara insists Japan’s market is open, uses figures Fraser sees as manipulated, and blames America’s problems on its workers and system. Fraser answers by attacking Ishihara’s authoritarianism and Japan’s lack of democracy.  
Payoff: The polite surface collapses. Each side’s private contempt becomes explicit.

**The Tennessee Decision**  
Setup: After years of delay and ambiguous talk, Nissan says it will build in the American South.  
Action: The plant becomes a truck factory in Tennessee, not the northern, unionized auto plant Shioji and the UAW wanted.  
Payoff: Nissan gains political cover by producing in America while still resisting the UAW.

## 5. Cast And Terms

- **Takashi Ishihara**: Nissan president after Kawamata; ambitious, isolated, financially oriented, wary of American labor, and determined to control the company.
- **Katsuji Kawamata**: Former Nissan president and later chairman; associated with Nissan’s earlier rise and with Shioji’s influence inside the company.
- **Ichiro Shioji**: Powerful Nissan union leader with deep UAW ties; wants a unionized American Nissan plant but loses leverage under Ishihara.
- **Pat Greathouse**: UAW international affairs official; frequent visitor to Japan and advocate for Japanese production in America.
- **Doug Fraser**: UAW president; more publicly confrontational with Ishihara and later deeply angered by Nissan’s anti-UAW posture.
- **Masataka Okuma**: Nissan executive vice-president whose remarks Greathouse interprets as encouraging signs of a future U.S. plant.
- **UAW**: United Auto Workers, the American union seeking to preserve jobs and organize any Japanese factory in the United States.
- **Mike Mansfield**: U.S. ambassador to Japan who encourages American and Japanese leaders to address mutual economic tensions.
- **250,000-car rule**: Greathouse’s proposed threshold: companies selling more than 250,000 cars a year in America should build there.
- **No-fault internationalism**: A description of Nissan’s approach to overseas plants: choose locations eager enough to offer generous terms while avoiding deeper obligations.

## 6. What To Hold Lightly

- Ishihara’s family and adoption story helps define his odd personal distance, but it is not central to the factory decision.
- The nickname “The Island” captures Ishihara’s isolation; remember the trait more than the phrase.
- The International Harvester anecdote matters mainly as evidence of Ishihara’s habit of cutting around colleagues.
- The exact car-count dispute in Fraser’s meeting matters less than what it reveals: both sides believe the other is manipulating the conversation.
- Shioji’s line about Ishihara looking like a lion with “a heart the size of a pea” is vivid, but the structural point is his loss of power.
