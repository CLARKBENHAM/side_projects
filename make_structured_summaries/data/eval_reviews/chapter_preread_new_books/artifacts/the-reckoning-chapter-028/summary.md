## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter runs from the mid-1950s through early 1974, tracing how cheap oil stopped being a stable assumption for the United States, Europe, and Japan.
- The main conflict is not simply “Arabs versus the West.” It is a shift in bargaining power: oil-producing states discover that the companies need them more than they had believed.
- The old order rests on the major oil companies controlling posted prices, access to markets, and producer-country leverage. That control depends on unity among the companies.
- Arab nationalism, anti-colonial anger, and resentment over Western support for Israel keep building beneath what looks like a stable oil market.
- Saudi Arabia and Iran matter most because they are seen as conservative, Western-friendly states, yet they become central actors in forcing the price revolution.
- Watch the distinction between the political weapon of embargo and the economic fact of rising market price. Halberstam treats both as converging forces.
- The American car culture is not background color. It is the vulnerable end point of decades of cheap-energy assumptions.

## The Chapter Arc

- Middle Eastern nationalism moves from background resentment to a real constraint on Western power after Nasser, Suez, and the decline of overt colonial intervention.
- The oil companies maintain apparent stability by keeping posted prices near $2 a barrel, but that stability increasingly depends on suppressing normal market signals.
- The 1967 Six-Day War exposes Arab military weakness and makes the first oil weapon look ineffective, but that failure pushes Arab states toward greater cooperation.
- Global demand changes the balance: Europe, Japan, and developing countries become oil-dependent just as American reserves flatten and U.S. imports rise.
- Qaddafi’s Libya breaks the old company discipline by isolating Occidental, proving that a producer state can pick off a vulnerable firm.
- Armand Hammer’s failure to get Exxon’s protection cracks the cartel’s solidarity, turning one company’s weakness into a broader structural breach.
- OPEC countries gain confidence through new price concessions in Libya and Iran, and the oil producers begin to act as a counter-oligopoly to the companies.
- The October 1973 war fuses price conflict with Arab anger over American support for Israel, turning a tense negotiation into an embargo.
- By early 1974 the embargo ends, but the price revolution remains: oil has quadrupled, the companies are junior partners, and American life has been briefly but sharply exposed.

## What To Watch For

- Halberstam’s central mechanism is leverage discovered late: the producer countries had power before they fully believed they had it.
- The posted price is a political instrument, not a neutral market number. The companies use it to stabilize profits and contain producer demands.
- Company unity is essential to Western control. Once Qaddafi finds a weak link in Occidental, the system’s apparent solidity starts to fail.
- Anti-colonial humiliation matters as much as revenue. The oil states want money, but also recognition, control, and release from the “mystical power” of the companies.
- Saudi Arabia’s position is delicate: it is conservative and friendly to the West, but cannot openly break Arab solidarity without risking its legitimacy.
- Yamani is the chapter’s crucial broker. He wants more power for oil producers but fears a price so high it damages the world economy and, eventually, oil producers too.
- The Shah is treated as both America’s client and a resentful nationalist. His Western alignment does not prevent him from pushing prices upward.
- The American response is marked by misreading: officials and companies assume Arab division will prevent effective collective action.
- The final American failure is institutional memory. Once the immediate crisis passes, the country slides back toward cheap-oil habits instead of rebuilding around scarcity.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Suez and the end of old intervention:** Nasser seizes the canal, and Britain, France, and Israel move militarily against him. American pressure forces them to stop. The payoff is a new Middle East in which Western economic interests remain huge, but Western military freedom is much narrower.

**The failed 1967 oil weapon:** After Israel’s victory in the Six-Day War, some Arab states try to shut down production. The Saudis lose revenue and hurt themselves more than their enemies. The failure matters because it teaches the producers that anger alone is useless without coordination.

**Qaddafi isolates Occidental:** Libya’s new regime demands more for its oil and targets Occidental, the weakest company in the Libyan field. Hammer seeks help from Exxon, but Exxon refuses. The payoff is the first major rupture in the oil-company front.

**Yamani at Geneva in October 1973:** OPEC negotiators demand a major price increase while Egypt and Syria are at war with Israel. The companies offer only modest concessions. The Arabs leave, signaling that the era of company-dictated pricing is over.

**The Saudi warning in Washington:** Arab foreign ministers come to warn the United States about a possible boycott. Nixon does not meet them, and Omar Saqquaf’s anger becomes part of the diplomatic failure. The payoff is that American condescension helps harden the Arab position.

**The gas lines at home:** The embargo reaches ordinary Americans through scarcity, rising prices, long waits, fights, bribes, and panic buying. Halberstam uses this scene to show that cheap oil had been built into daily life, not merely into corporate balance sheets.

## Cast And Terms

**Gamal Abdel Nasser:** Egyptian nationalist leader who symbolizes the rise of pan-Arab politics after 1954.

**Suez Canal crisis:** The 1956 confrontation that shows old colonial powers can no longer freely impose outcomes in the Middle East.

**Six-Day War:** The 1967 Arab-Israeli war; Israel’s rapid victory deepens Arab anger and exposes Arab weakness.

**OPEC:** Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries; becomes the vehicle through which producers coordinate pricing power.

**Posted price:** The official price used by oil companies and producer governments, long kept artificially stable by company control.

**Market price:** The actual price buyers are willing to pay; by 1973 it rises above the posted price, revealing the old system’s collapse.

**The cartel / seven sisters:** The major international oil companies that had dominated production, pricing, and access to markets.

**Muammar Qaddafi:** Libyan officer who overthrows King Idris in 1969 and uses Libya’s oil position to force price concessions.

**Occidental Petroleum:** Independent oil company led by Armand Hammer; vulnerable in Libya and central to the cartel’s break.

**Armand Hammer:** Head of Occidental, forced to concede to Qaddafi after failing to get support from Exxon.

**John Jamieson:** Head of Exxon, refuses to protect Hammer, helping fracture company solidarity.

**Sheik Ahmed Yamani:** Saudi oil minister and OPEC spokesman; skilled negotiator balancing Arab solidarity, Saudi caution, and Western ties.

**The Shah of Iran:** Western-backed ruler who nevertheless becomes an aggressive critic of company power and a driver of higher oil prices.

**Omar Saqquaf:** Saudi foreign minister who helps deliver the boycott warning to Washington.

**Yom Kippur War:** The October 1973 war in which Egypt and Syria attack Israeli-held territory; it triggers the political context for the embargo.

## What To Hold Lightly

- Qaddafi’s bans on liquor, Latin print, and non-Arabic words are vivid signs of his anti-Western posture, but the key point is his leverage over fragmented Libyan concessions.
- The Bechtel workers calling Libyan workers by payroll numbers is useful atmosphere for colonial contempt, not the chapter’s main causal hinge.
- The gas-station anecdotes, including topping off and fights in line, dramatize the shock but matter less than the structural dependence they reveal.
- The exact auction bids in Iran and Algeria are less important than the fact that posted price and market price had fully separated.
