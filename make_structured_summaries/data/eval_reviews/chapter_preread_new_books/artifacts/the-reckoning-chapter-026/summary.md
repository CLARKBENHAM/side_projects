## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in the mid-1960s through 1968, as Nissan’s American operation moves from fragile importer to serious force in the U.S. small-car market.
- Yutaka Katayama is the central figure: Nissan’s American chief, culturally freer in the United States than in Japan, and unusually close to dealers, customers, journalists, and car enthusiasts.
- The core conflict is between market knowledge in America and bureaucratic caution in Tokyo. Katayama knows what U.S. buyers need; headquarters often resists because Japan’s domestic assumptions still dominate.
- Detroit is still rich and confident, but it is misreading a shift in customer resentment. Buyers are no longer objecting only to price and size; they are reacting to quality problems and dismissive service.
- Volkswagen is the dominant import power, but its reliance on the Beetle creates an opening. Nissan and Toyota are learning faster than VW and Detroit realize.
- The chapter’s hinge is the fight for a 1600cc engine, which turns the Datsun 510 from a decent small car into the breakthrough product Katayama has been trying to create.

## The Chapter Arc

- Katayama begins as the effective face of Nissan in America, not merely a branch manager. His daily intimacy with dealers and customers gives the American operation a personality Tokyo cannot supply.
- His American freedom changes his method. He rejects the insulated habits of Japanese businessmen abroad and builds knowledge by traveling alone, talking directly, and absorbing local tastes.
- His dealer network becomes a source of intelligence and loyalty. The dealers are not just sales outlets; they are proof that Datsun can create wealth for outsiders willing to take a risk.
- Nissan’s early American marketing starts crude and cheap, then becomes more confident as sales rise. Small monthly milestones turn into national visibility.
- Katayama’s critique of Detroit sharpens: the Big Three can build compacts, but their dealers and institutions lack the incentive to love them, support them, or treat small-car customers seriously.
- Imports cross from nuisance to structural threat. Detroit’s old assumption that it can crush imports whenever they exceed 5 percent begins to fail as buyer loyalty shifts toward quality and trust.
- Katayama pushes for more engine power and hits Tokyo’s resistance. The issue becomes bigger than horsepower: it reveals whether Nissan can design for America rather than merely export Japanese assumptions.
- Keiichi Matsumura’s arrival changes the internal politics. His MITI authority and independence give Katayama a channel strong enough to force the 1600cc question onto the board agenda.
- The Datsun 510 arrives as Katayama’s vindication. It proves Nissan can combine European-style performance, Japanese manufacturing, and a mass-market price, accelerating Japan’s arrival as an automotive power.

## What To Watch For

- Katayama’s authority is informal but powerful. He lacks Tokyo rank, money, and entourage, yet he controls the American reality that matters: dealers, customers, taste, and feedback.
- Halberstam treats cultural adaptation as a business advantage. Katayama succeeds partly because he does not merely sell in America; he studies America as a lived environment.
- Dealer incentives matter throughout. Detroit dealers dislike compacts because small cars threaten full-size profits, while Datsun dealers depend on the import business and therefore push it hard.
- “Listening” is not sentimental here. It means converting customer complaints, dealer reports, magazine reviews, and market behavior into product decisions before competitors do.
- The 1600cc engine fight is a mechanism of institutional change. A technical request succeeds only when Katayama finds a political sponsor strong enough to overcome headquarters inertia.
- MITI’s role is indirect but important. Matsumura comes from the state bureaucracy, yet his authority inside Nissan helps cut through the company’s own bureaucratic timidity.
- The 510 matters because it changes the meaning of “small car.” It is not just economical; it is sporty, durable, technically modern, and aspirational for a new kind of buyer.
- Watch how Halberstam contrasts Detroit’s confidence with its blindness. American automakers are profitable enough to dismiss the evidence that poorer and younger buyers are defecting for reasons of respect and integrity.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Katayama alone at headquarters:** A customer needs a part on a Saturday. Katayama finds it himself and charges a token dollar because he does not know the price. The scene establishes his hands-on style and the intimacy of Nissan’s early American operation.

**Japanese businessmen at lunch:** Katayama sees Japanese managers eating only with one another and calls them afraid to be in America. The action crystallizes his contempt for insularity and explains why his market understanding is different from Tokyo’s.

**The Matsumura telex:** After Katayama explains the need for a 1600cc engine, Matsumura tells him to write a telex for Matsumura to sign. The message shocks Nissan headquarters and breaks the stalemate, showing how truth from the market needs power behind it.

**The first 510 comes off the ship:** Katayama waits sleeplessly, then drives the white car with red interior out of the San Pedro facility. His joy is personal, but the payoff is strategic: the car proves Japan’s engineers can build the kind of product he had imagined.

**The Baroque commercial:** Katayama rejects feature-heavy advertising and helps create a silent, Vivaldi-scored ad showing the 510 driving through Big Sur rain. The scene shows his belief that the car’s performance can speak for itself.

**VW dealers approach Datsun:** Soon after the 510 appears, Volkswagen dealers want to switch or add Datsun. The payoff is market confirmation: Nissan is not merely gaining sales; it is taking prestige and momentum from the import leader.

## Cast And Terms

- **Yutaka Katayama:** Head of Nissan’s American operation; later famous as the father of Datsun in the U.S. market.
- **Nissan / Datsun:** Nissan is the Japanese company; Datsun is the brand name used for its cars in the American market.
- **Ishihara:** Tokyo-based Nissan figure responsible for America; useful to Katayama but not his real operating partner.
- **Kawamata:** Nissan president; powerful, conservative, and often the source of Tokyo’s product and naming decisions.
- **Shioji:** Nissan power figure associated with the internal political order Katayama finds oppressive.
- **Keiichi Matsumura:** Former MITI automobile official who joins Nissan and becomes Katayama’s crucial ally on the 1600cc engine.
- **MITI:** Japan’s Ministry of International Trade and Industry, a powerful state ministry deeply involved in industrial policy.
- **Yuji Shimamoto:** Export department official who had resisted Katayama’s engine requests before the Matsumura intervention.
- **Mayfield Marshall:** Katayama friend and speechwriter; used by Halberstam as a witness to Katayama’s personality.
- **John Parker:** Young Los Angeles advertising man who builds Datsun’s early low-budget American campaigns.
- **Datsun 510:** The 1968 breakthrough sedan with a 1600cc engine, independent suspension, strong performance, and low price.
- **BMW 1600:** German model admired by Katayama and effectively the performance template the 510 makes affordable.
- **Volkswagen Beetle / Bug:** Dominant import car in America before Japanese makers begin taking share.
- **Big Three:** General Motors, Ford, and Chrysler, still dominant but increasingly complacent.

## What To Hold Lightly

- Katayama’s kite flying, hiking, fishing, painting, and Christmas-card habits are vivid personality markers, but the central point is his immersion in American life.
- The comic naming disputes over Bluebonnet, Cedric, Fair Lady, Lion, and Tiger matter mostly as evidence of Tokyo’s poor feel for American taste.
- The Roy Rogers commercial is useful color for Datsun’s scrappy marketing, not a main turning point.
- The “suitcases” joke about lawsuits shows Katayama’s one real American fear, but it is secondary to the product and market story.
