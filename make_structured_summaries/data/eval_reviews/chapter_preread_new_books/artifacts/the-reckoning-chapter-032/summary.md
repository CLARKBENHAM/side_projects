## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter begins with the 1973 oil shock, which hits Japan as an existential vulnerability rather than just a consumer inconvenience: Japan has built a modern industrial economy on oil it does not possess.
- Nissan and Toyota enter the crisis with a structural advantage: years of work on smaller, efficient, better-built cars now match the American market’s sudden fear of gasoline prices and shortages.
- Yutaka Katayama, head of Nissan’s American operation, is near the peak of his achievement. He has made Datsun credible in America, but that success also makes him more exposed to Tokyo.
- The American car companies face a strategic crisis: their large-car model is suddenly under pressure, while no one knows whether the gas panic is temporary or permanent.
- Volkswagen, once the dominant import, is caught flat-footed. Its Beetle-centered strategy and exchange-rate problems leave it vulnerable just as Japanese imports surge.
- Inside Nissan, the conflict shifts from market competition to corporate politics: Katayama’s American fame, informality, and independence become liabilities at headquarters.
- The chapter’s title, “Datsun Saves,” works both as an advertising slogan and as Halberstam’s larger claim: the oil crisis saves Japanese automakers in America, but not necessarily the man most responsible for Datsun’s American success.

## The Chapter Arc

- Japan’s reaction to the oil shock changes the crisis from a foreign-policy event into a national panic, exposing how dependent the country’s prosperity is on imported energy.
- Emergency conservation measures turn Japanese industry inward, forcing companies to rework production habits and making discipline under scarcity part of the national response.
- The oil shock alters the competitive meaning of the small Japanese car: what had looked modest or underpowered now becomes timely, practical, and desirable.
- Nissan’s planned upscale “Datsun Originals” campaign is overtaken by events; a status-oriented message gives way to a blunt fuel-economy pitch.
- The Datsun 1200’s strong mileage test changes a nearly obsolete small car into proof of Nissan’s relevance, and Katayama immediately makes “Datsun Saves” the campaign.
- The embargo damages everyone at first, including the Japanese, but by 1975 the crisis has changed the import hierarchy: Toyota passes Volkswagen, and Japanese cars take a larger share of the American market.
- Katayama’s success changes his standing inside Nissan from useful independence to political danger. The more visible he becomes in America, the harder Tokyo moves to contain him.
- Tokyo begins reclaiming the American operation by sending executives, then installs Hiroshi Majima above Katayama, signaling that the era of Katayama’s autonomy is ending.
- Katayama is abruptly recalled, retired with little ceremony, denied the status his achievement would normally command, and remembered more generously by American car culture than by Nissan itself.

## What To Watch For

- The oil shock is not just a demand-side event. It affects production, steel supply, shipping, factory energy use, exchange rates, and consumer psychology all at once.
- Halberstam presents Japanese vulnerability as a source of competitive strength: scarcity forces discipline, conservation, and attention to efficiency before Americans fully adapt.
- The yen’s fall matters because it makes exports easier, compounding the advantage Japanese automakers already have from fuel-efficient cars.
- The chapter contrasts two kinds of preparedness: Detroit has scale and market dominance, while Japan has the right product at the right moment.
- Katayama’s instincts are shown through advertising decisions. He recognizes that the emotional center of the market has changed and abandons a prestige campaign for a utility campaign.
- Volkswagen’s decline is not treated as inevitable. It results from product aging, price pressure, exchange rates, and Japanese cars becoming better in the same market segment.
- Corporate credit is a central mechanism. Katayama’s American reputation offends Nissan’s hierarchy because it implies personal authorship in a system that expects deference upward.
- “Going American” is both Katayama’s strength and his vulnerability. His closeness to American dealers, customers, homes, manners, and media helps him win the market but worsens his position in Tokyo.
- Watch how Halberstam uses small social rituals, especially meetings, houses, watches, and parties, to show institutional judgment without needing formal accusations.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Japan in the dark after the oil shock**  
Setup: Japan has no domestic oil and suddenly sees its industrial future threatened.  
Action: Companies cut energy use, stores are mobbed, hot water disappears from lavatories, and office hallways are dimmed.  
Payoff: The scene makes the crisis physical and national, not abstract; Japan’s later advantage comes from having felt the danger more deeply.

**The abandoned art campaign**  
Setup: Nissan is preparing to push the 610 upscale with famous artists and a “Datsun Originals” theme.  
Action: Dealers dislike the status angle, and the oil crisis changes what buyers care about.  
Payoff: The episode shows how quickly market meaning can reverse: luxury aspiration loses force, mileage becomes the selling point.

**The Datsun 1200 crossing America**  
Setup: Nissan learns that the aging 1200 has topped the Department of Transportation mileage test.  
Action: Mayfield Marshall films it driving from California to Maine, with independent authorities certifying high mileage.  
Payoff: The commercial’s final line, “Datsun Saves,” gives Nissan a simple, perfectly timed message for the embargo era.

**Katayama goes “across the bridge”**  
Setup: Friends urge Katayama to repair his relationship with Shioji, the powerful Nissan figure he has not courted.  
Action: Katayama finally visits; Shioji makes him wait an hour.  
Payoff: The delay is the message. Katayama’s success has not erased the hierarchy he ignored.

**The Palos Verdes house**  
Setup: Katayama buys a modest-by-American-standards house and uses it for American-style entertaining.  
Action: Visiting Nissan executives see it as excessive and suspect high living.  
Payoff: The house becomes evidence against him because Tokyo cannot or will not translate American business customs into Japanese corporate terms.

**The gold watch**  
Setup: Katayama is summoned home without explanation and effectively retired before his American colleagues know what has happened.  
Action: Mayfield Marshall cables, hoping Nissan gives him more than a gold watch; Katayama later returns and silently shows the watch on his wrist.  
Payoff: The gesture compresses Nissan’s treatment of him: immense achievement, minimal gratitude.

## Cast And Terms

- **Yutaka Katayama / Mr. K**: Head of Nissan’s American operation; central figure behind Datsun’s rise in the United States.
- **Nissan / Datsun**: Nissan is the Japanese company; Datsun is the brand name used in the American market.
- **Datsun 1200**: Small, older Nissan model whose fuel economy becomes unexpectedly valuable during the oil crisis.
- **Datsun 210**: Low-cost replacement that benefits from the same fuel-economy moment.
- **610**: Nissan’s more upscale model, intended to help the company move upmarket before the crisis shifts the advertising strategy.
- **Mayfield Marshall**: Advertising figure and close Katayama ally; helps create the “Datsun Saves” commercial.
- **Yom Kippur War**: 1973 Arab-Israeli war that precedes the Arab oil embargo.
- **Oil embargo**: Arab oil-producing countries’ cutoff that sharply changes fuel prices, supply expectations, and car-buying behavior.
- **Volkswagen / Beetle**: Former import leader in America; weakened by product limitations and a strong deutsche mark.
- **Shioji**: Powerful Nissan figure tied to internal politics; someone Katayama is urged to appease.
- **Kawazoe**: Katayama’s East Coast counterpart, less successful in sales but better connected politically.
- **Ishihara, Kawamata, Okuma**: Senior Nissan figures whose visits signal Tokyo’s scrutiny of the American operation.
- **Hiroshi Majima**: Tokyo-backed executive sent to America in 1975; his arrival marks the end of Katayama’s control.
- **MITI**: Japan’s Ministry of International Trade and Industry; awards Katayama a blue ribbon for trade work.
- **Car and Driver**: American car magazine that later treats Katayama as a near-mythic figure and criticizes Nissan’s post-Katayama dullness.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The exact titles and plots of the Japanese panic books matter less than what they reveal: national fear about isolation and vulnerability.
- The famous artists in the “Datsun Originals” campaign are colorful, but the key point is that the campaign’s whole premise becomes obsolete.
- Katayama’s honorary sheriff status and sporty clothes are useful texture, not the core cause of his removal.
- The later resale value of the Palos Verdes house is ironic detail; the important issue is how differently American and Japanese executives interpreted it.
