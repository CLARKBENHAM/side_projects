## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits inside Toyota’s postwar climb from a constrained Japanese automaker into a production system able to handle variety, low inventories, and volatile demand.
- The central institution is Toyota Motor Company, but the system eventually has to include cooperating outside firms, especially parts suppliers around Nagoya and farther away in Kanto.
- Taiichi Ohno is not presenting kanban as a clever scheduling card. He is describing a management system that changes authority, habits, plant layout, supplier relations, and the meaning of “common sense.”
- The main conflict is between planned mass production, which rewards large batches and early production, and just-in-time production, which demands exact timing, small lots, and exposed problems.
- Supermarkets provide the analogy: customers withdraw only what they need, and replenishment follows actual use. Toyota turns that consumer-retail logic into a factory-control mechanism.
- The stakes are practical, not abstract: overproduction, warehouses, idle indirect labor, defective work, long setup times, supplier confusion, and the burden placed on earlier processes by uneven final assembly demand.

## The Chapter Arc

- Ohno begins with the supermarket model becoming concrete: American-style supermarkets arrive in Japan, then his 1956 U.S. visit confirms the analogy he had been pursuing.
- The chapter shifts from inspiration to mechanism: kanban becomes the operating method that carries pickup, transfer, and production information through Toyota and its suppliers.
- The old planning logic is reversed. Instead of central schedules pushing work forward, later processes withdraw from earlier ones only when needed.
- This reversal creates new instability. Earlier processes lose the comfort of large batches and fixed schedules, forcing Toyota to confront setup time, lot size, and uneven withdrawal.
- Ohno’s personal authority expands alongside the system: machine shop experiments in 1949-1950 grow into Motomachi trials, then company-wide adoption in 1962.
- Toyota learns that kanban cannot be copied onto an unprepared plant. Flow, standard work, and production leveling must be established first.
- After internal adoption, Toyota begins teaching nearby cooperating firms by demonstration rather than textbook explanation, then gradually extends the method outward.
- The chapter broadens from kanban rules to production leveling: Toyota’s final assembly line must smooth its own demand so earlier processes and suppliers are not whiplashed.
- The final movement shows kanban’s elasticity through carts, conveyors, and irregular balance weights, arguing that kanban is a principle of controlled communication, not merely a paper card.

## What To Watch For

- Watch the reversal of direction: later processes go to earlier processes. This is the first rule of kanban and the conceptual break from conventional production flow.
- Notice how “just” in just-in-time is treated literally. Early parts are not harmless; they create storage, management work, hidden defects, and waste.
- Track the difference between the Toyota Production System and kanban. Ohno insists TPS is the production method; kanban is the management tool that operates it.
- Watch for the sequence of prerequisites: flow first, leveling next, standard work throughout, then kanban as the visible control system.
- Pay attention to how exposing problems is part of the design. If a defect stops the next process, the earlier process “feels the pinch,” making recurrence harder to ignore.
- Notice that production leveling places responsibility on final assembly. If the final process creates peaks and valleys, every upstream process needs excess capacity.
- Watch Ohno’s treatment of authority. Kanban spreads only where he has enough jurisdiction to test, pressure, teach, and protect the experiment.
- Watch the attack on dedicated equipment. What once lowered cost under mass production can become a constraint when variety and small lots matter more.
- Notice the recurring warning: kanban used without the underlying production changes becomes dangerous, especially when forced onto outside suppliers.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Supermarket as factory model.**  
Setup: Toyota is searching for a way to replace only what has actually been used. Action: Ohno studies the supermarket, where customer purchases trigger replenishment. Payoff: withdrawal kanban and production kanban become the factory version of shelves, checkout information, and restocking.

**The machine shop trial around 1953.**  
Setup: Toyota needs a practical way to connect machining work with actual downstream need. Action: pieces of paper list part numbers and machining information, then move with parts. Payoff: kanban becomes a simple information carrier capable of coordinating quantity, timing, sequence, destination, and containers.

**The first rule meets resistance.**  
Setup: Earlier processes are used to schedules and large batches. Action: Toyota asks them to produce only what later processes withdraw. Payoff: the rule forces setup reduction, smaller lots, and management commitment because workers no longer get credit for simply making as much as possible.

**Company-wide adoption in 1962.**  
Setup: For years, kanban can only be tested inside Ohno’s sphere of authority, avoiding processes that would disrupt the whole plant. Action: once Ohno manages the main plant, forging and casting are brought into the system. Payoff: kanban earns recognition just before Toyota enters a high-growth period.

**Tsutsumi production leveling.**  
Setup: Corona, Carina, and Celica production could easily be batched by model or body type. Action: Toyota alternates models and sequences sedans, hardtops, and wagons in small repeated patterns. Payoff: final assembly smooths demand so upstream processes avoid large swings.

**Balance weights for propeller shafts.**  
Setup: five tiny parts are used irregularly and cannot be predicted cleanly by a production plan. Action: kanban is attached to the actual weights so type and quantity are always visible. Payoff: even unstable, special-use parts can be controlled without urgent shortages or excess inventory.

## Cast And Terms

**Taiichi Ohno**: Toyota executive and architect of the Toyota Production System; the chapter’s narrator and main experimenter.

**Toyota Motor Company**: the automaker where kanban is developed, tested, and eventually made company-wide.

**Cooperating firms**: Toyota’s outside suppliers, gradually taught Toyota methods and later kanban.

**Kanban**: usually a paper card in a vinyl envelope carrying pickup, transfer, and production information; more broadly, a control signal tied to actual goods.

**Just-in-time**: producing and delivering only the needed item, at the needed time, in the needed quantity.

**Withdrawal kanban**: signal authorizing a later process to pick up needed goods from an earlier process.

**Production kanban**: signal authorizing the earlier process to produce what has been withdrawn.

**Production leveling / load smoothing**: reducing peaks and valleys in production mix and quantity so upstream processes can operate without excess capacity or inventory.

**Earlier process / later process**: upstream and downstream production steps; kanban reverses control by letting later processes withdraw from earlier ones.

**Muda, mura, muri**: waste, inconsistency, and unreasonableness; defects in work organization that eventually create defective products.

**Motomachi, Tsutsumi, Nagoya, Kanto**: Toyota plant or regional locations used to show how the system spread unevenly across plants and suppliers.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The exact dollar figure for Toyota’s annual production is less important than the claim that kanban controlled a very large, complex operation.
- The precise monthly car counts and body-style ratios matter mainly as an illustration of sequencing, not as numbers to memorize.
- The vinyl envelope form of kanban is vivid but secondary; the real point is attached, circulating production information.
- The personal complaints against Ohno matter as evidence of resistance, but the deeper issue is the organizational difficulty of reversing production logic.
