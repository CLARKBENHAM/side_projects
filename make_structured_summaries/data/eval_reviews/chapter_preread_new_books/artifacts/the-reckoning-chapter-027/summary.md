## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in the early 1970s, after Nissan has survived its hardest labor fights and is moving into the boom years of Japanese auto expansion.
- Ichiro Shioji, once a union man, has become one of the most powerful figures inside Nissan: part labor leader, part political operator, part enforcer for management.
- The central conflict is not a strike or a public confrontation, but the consolidation of power over Nissan’s supplier network.
- MITI wants Japan’s auto suppliers stabilized and rationalized: fewer weak shops, closer ties to big assemblers, better financing, better quality.
- Nissan’s management wants the same outcome for practical reasons: predictable parts, lower costs, fewer disruptions, and tighter control over quality.
- Shioji becomes the instrument that makes this system work. He disciplines supplier owners, supplier workers, and even unions that are supposed to be independent.
- Halberstam is also tracking a personal transformation: Shioji’s rise from useful outsider to near-imperial insider, with the question of whether such power will eventually make him vulnerable.

## The Chapter Arc

- Shioji’s public manner changes: he becomes more worldly, more confident, more visibly connected to foreign labor leaders, politicians, and international institutions.
- His personal display of power grows into a corporate signal: gifts from abroad, golf, a yacht crewed by Nissan employees, and constant access to political circles all make him seem larger than his formal title.
- The chapter reframes Nissan’s modernization as dependent not only on engineering and capital, but on disciplining the supplier base.
- The suppliers move from precarious independent shops toward a structured hierarchy under Nissan, with ownership stakes giving the assembler practical control.
- What first looks like a business rescue for small suppliers becomes a loss of autonomy: owners discover that Nissan’s protection also means obedience.
- Shioji extends labor discipline beyond Nissan’s own plants, teaching supplier presidents and workers that they sit below Nissan in the industrial order.
- Hideo Kuze’s attempt to run the Suppliers Union with some independence becomes a test case, and Shioji destroys him as a warning to others.
- With Kuze removed, Nissan completes its control over supplier labor, making strikes and cost surprises far less likely.
- The chapter ends by turning Shioji’s own logic back on him: the man who hammered down dissidents may himself have become conspicuous enough to be hammered later.

## What To Watch For

- Watch how labor power is converted into management power. Shioji’s authority comes from being able to represent workers while also controlling them.
- Notice the difference between formal hierarchy and real hierarchy. Board members outrank Shioji on paper, but people behave as if he is the man who matters.
- Pay attention to supplier rationalization as an industrial mechanism, not just a personnel story. Nissan needs suppliers modern enough to raise quality but dependent enough to obey.
- The stock formulas matter: 90 percent ownership for critical parts, 40 percent for less critical ones. These numbers show how control is calibrated by production risk.
- Halberstam keeps linking Japanese industrial success to coercive social ordering: modernization arrives through discipline, deference, and exclusion as much as through technology.
- Golf, dinner seating, cocktails, lateness, and gifts are not decorative details here. They are rituals through which rank is displayed and accepted.
- The Suppliers Union is a crucial pressure point because Nissan cannot fully control production if supplier workers can strike independently.
- Kuze’s fall shows that Shioji’s system cannot tolerate even mild autonomy among protégés.
- Suzuki’s final reflection supplies the chapter’s moral hinge: Shioji is powerful because he is the hammer, but power may also make him the next nail.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Shioji on the yacht**  
Setup: His friends already see him becoming more international, more confident, and more important.  
Action: He hosts them on a large yacht with a crew of Nissan employees working on their days off.  
Payoff: The image is unmistakable: Shioji does not merely work inside Nissan; he commands people and resources as if Nissan itself were an extension of him.

**The supplier ownership formula**  
Setup: Japanese auto suppliers are underfinanced, unstable, and uneven in quality, threatening the growth of the major assemblers.  
Action: Nissan begins taking controlling or decisive stakes in suppliers, depending on how vital their parts are to production.  
Payoff: Modernization becomes possible, but independence disappears. The small shops gain security by entering Nissan’s hierarchy.

**The argument with Akira Sugita**  
Setup: Shioji visits a supplier and decides the workers have not shown him enough deference.  
Action: Sugita defends them, saying they are young and that bonus levels are his own decision, not worker rebellion.  
Payoff: Other supplier presidents understand the danger better than Sugita does: their role is not to argue with Shioji, but to say yes.

**Shioji makes everyone wait while he eats**  
Setup: A supplier meeting includes major executives and financiers, and Shioji is scheduled to speak.  
Action: He arrives late, refuses to hurry in, and eats first while the supplier presidents wait.  
Payoff: The delay is the message. He can impose inconvenience and humiliation because everyone depends on Nissan’s favor.

**Kuze is squeezed out**  
Setup: Hideo Kuze, Shioji’s protégé, is placed at the head of the Suppliers Union and expected to follow instructions.  
Action: Kuze shows signs of independence; Shioji undercuts him by negotiating contracts himself, then uses management support to force him out.  
Payoff: The Suppliers Union’s independence collapses, and Nissan’s control over supplier labor becomes complete.

## Cast And Terms

**Ichiro Shioji**: Nissan labor leader and power broker. He functions as Kawamata’s enforcer across labor, suppliers, and internal politics.

**Kawamata**: Senior Nissan executive whose authority Shioji helps secure. He becomes more distant as Shioji becomes the visible man to deal with.

**MITI**: Japan’s Ministry of International Trade and Industry. It pushes supplier stabilization and rationalization as part of strengthening the auto industry.

**Supplier companies**: Smaller firms that make parts for Nissan and other assemblers. Many begin as fragile family firms and become dependent Nissan affiliates.

**Suppliers Union**: Union for workers at supplier companies. Formally separate from Shioji’s Japan Auto Workers, but eventually brought under his control.

**Hideo Kuze**: Shioji protégé placed in charge of the Suppliers Union. His limited independence leads to his removal and personal collapse.

**Akira Sugita**: Supplier-company owner who argues with Shioji and briefly resists the expected posture of deference.

**Takashi Suzuki**: Former Prince labor union leader defeated by Shioji. He provides the final reflection on Shioji as both hammer and possible future nail.

**Prince merger**: Earlier Nissan absorption of Prince, helped by Shioji’s labor maneuvering. It is part of his record as Nissan’s fixer.

**“The nail that stands out gets hammered in”**: Japanese proverb used here to describe how dissenters or conspicuous challengers are disciplined.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The names of every American labor figure Shioji mentions are less important than what they signal: he now sees himself as an international figure.
- The personalized lighters, golf clubs, Scotch, and cocktail mixing are vivid status markers, not separate plot points.
- The exact etiquette of dinner seating matters mainly as evidence of rank and flattery.
- The yacht details matter less as luxury than as a scene showing how corporate labor and personal authority have blurred around Shioji.
