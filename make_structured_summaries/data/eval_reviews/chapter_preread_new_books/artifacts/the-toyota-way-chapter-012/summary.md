## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter moves Toyota Production System ideas out of the factory and into service, technical, engineering, accounting, postal, and administrative work.
- The central problem is visibility: in services, waste often hides as information waiting in queues, unclear handoffs, rework, approvals, and uncoordinated schedules.
- Liker argues that service work is less mysterious than it looks. Many processes that seem unique turn out to be repetitive enough to map, standardize, and improve.
- The main institutional examples are Canada Post, Northrop Grumman Ship Systems, Genie Industries, and Toyota’s Georgetown accounting setup.
- The conflict is between “fast local work” and slow total flow: a department may process its own task quickly while the full customer process sits idle for hours or days.
- The practical method is value stream mapping followed by kaizen workshops, visual control, measurable follow-through, and PDCA learning.

## The Chapter Arc

- The chapter begins by reframing service work as flow: instead of tracking physical parts, the reader is asked to track information, decisions, approvals, and customer value.
- Waste changes from an abstract complaint into a map-able delay: information inventory becomes the service equivalent of parts sitting between machines.
- One-piece flow is redefined less as a manufacturing trick than as a way to tightly link processes so delays and defects become immediately visible.
- Canada Post turns from a mail “warehouse” optimized around huge sorting machines into a case for reducing travel, staging, storage, and lead time across the whole postal flow.
- The method then shifts from case story to operating recipe: define customers, separate repetitive from unique work, map current and future states, then implement through PDCA.
- Kaizen workshops become the chapter’s core mechanism: a temporary cross-functional team maps, debates, redesigns, starts implementation, and sets up sustaining routines.
- Northrop Grumman shows that even small-looking engineering items, like ship label plates, can be large value streams when multiplied by volume and customer consequences.
- Genie Industries adds the control layer: visual boards replace hidden computer schedules so engineering work can be managed in plain sight.
- The chapter closes by making support functions subordinate to the core value stream: accounting, law, engineering, and other services should be designed around the real work they support.

## What To Watch For

- Watch the distinction between value-added, non-value-added, and required non-value-added work. Liker uses it to avoid both fake precision and political fights.
- Track how “inventory” changes meaning. In this chapter, inventory is often a drawing, form, decision, repair item, engineering change, or file waiting for attention.
- Notice that local speed is repeatedly shown as misleading. Canada Post sorted mail quickly, but the mail still spent most of its life waiting or moving.
- Pay attention to customer definition. The accounting example shows that one process may serve employees, the business, and regulators at the same time.
- Look for how layout and organization mirror flow. Supervisors, engineers, databases, work centers, and teams are reorganized around value streams rather than departments.
- Watch how measurement is used after implementation. Metrics are not decoration; they are meant to sustain gains and trigger management attention.
- Notice the bias toward starting with repetitive subprocesses. Liker does not claim every service activity is easy to standardize.
- Keep an eye on genchi genbutsu: teams are expected to walk the process, inspect real work, and talk to people doing it before redesigning anything.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Canada Post’s Ottawa Map**  
Setup: The Ottawa sorting facility appears productive because machines sort mail quickly.  
Action: The team maps the actual path of a letter and finds 167 meters of travel, eight storage-and-removal cycles, 26 hours of lead time, and only 12 seconds of value-added sorting.  
Payoff: The plant is recast as a warehouse, not a flow system, and redesign frees enough space to empty an entire floor.

**Hamilton’s Flow Cell**  
Setup: Each area in the Hamilton mail facility operates as its own work center, and the plant runs continuously while still missing customer commitments.  
Action: The facility creates continuous-flow cells, reassigns supervisors from process islands to flow cells, paces work in 15-minute intervals, then attacks layout and changeover time.  
Payoff: Smaller batches and faster changeovers reduce inventory and lead time, proving the problem was system design rather than worker effort.

**The “Parcel Hospital”**  
Setup: Damaged or underpaid items are sent to a separate rate-and-repair department, where off-shift problems may wait many hours.  
Action: Canada Post replaces the batch “hospital” with mobile rate-and-repair capability and flexible staffing on each shift.  
Payoff: Lead time drops, space is saved, customer satisfaction improves, and earlier flow improvements reduce the damage volume in the first place.

**The Accounting Customer Debate**  
Setup: In an accounting kaizen event, employee expense reimbursement looks simple until the team asks who the customer is.  
Action: The group considers the employee, the organization, and the IRS, then separates value from required non-value-added work.  
Payoff: The example shows why service improvement cannot begin with tools; it has to begin with customer value and constraints.

**Northrop Grumman Label Plates**  
Setup: Ship label plates seem minor because each plate is easy to make, but a ship may contain 40,000 of them and Navy approval depends on correctness.  
Action: A kaizen workshop moves label-plate comments earlier, standardizes work, consolidates data, and attacks root causes of rework.  
Payoff: Lead time falls 54%, rework falls 80%, productivity improves 29%, and daily stress drops because emergency replacements become rare.

## Cast And Terms

- **Jeffrey K. Liker**: Author; uses cases and workshop methods to extend Toyota Way principles beyond manufacturing.
- **TPS**: Toyota Production System; here treated as a broad operating philosophy, not only factory tools.
- **One-piece flow**: Moving work through linked steps without batching; in services, often means information moving without queues.
- **Information inventory**: Work waiting to be used or processed, such as forms, approvals, drawings, change notices, or data.
- **Value stream map**: A map of process steps, flows, queues, lead time, and value-added time.
- **Future-state map**: The proposed lean redesign of the value stream.
- **Kaizen workshop**: A short, structured improvement event with preparation, current-state analysis, future-state design, implementation, and follow-up.
- **Process owner**: The manager responsible for the process being improved.
- **Genchi genbutsu**: Going to the actual place to understand actual work.
- **PDCA**: Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle for testing, sustaining, and improving changes.
- **Steve Withers**: Canada Post lean leader cited for describing the old batch mentality.
- **Mike Young**: Hamilton plant director who sustains flow improvements.
- **John Drogosz**: Lean consultant associated with the Northrop Grumman label-plate event.
- **Glenn Uminger**: Toyota accountant who learns TPS firsthand before designing a simpler accounting system.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The exact diagrams and figure numbers matter less than the recurring pattern: current-state waste, future-state flow, then implementation.
- Some percentage improvements are vivid proof points, but the causal mechanisms matter more than memorizing each number.
- Canada Post’s scale statistics are useful context, not the chapter’s main argument.
- The specific tools on Genie’s boards matter less than the principle that work status becomes visible, shared, and reviewable.
