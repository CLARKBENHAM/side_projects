## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in the early-to-mid 1980s, when Nissan is losing ground to Toyota at home and trying to protect its future by manufacturing inside foreign markets rather than merely exporting into them.
- The central fight is between Takashi Ishihara, Nissan’s president, and Ichiro Shioji, the powerful union leader who has long exercised unusual influence inside the company.
- The immediate issue is whether Nissan will build a plant in Britain, but the deeper issue is who actually controls Nissan: the president, the chairman, the union boss, or the informal consensus system around them.
- Ishihara wants international expansion, especially into the European Common Market, where quotas and protectionist barriers make Japanese exports increasingly vulnerable.
- Shioji opposes the British plant partly on business grounds, partly because he distrusts British unions, and partly because the fight gives him a way to strike back after losing influence over Nissan’s American plant.
- Kawamata, Nissan’s aging chairman, becomes the swing figure. His hesitation gives Shioji cover; his eventual shift gives Ishihara the leverage he needs.
- The chapter shows Japanese corporate “harmony” under stress: the conflict is not a clean boardroom vote or a formal strike, but a long struggle of protocol, press leaks, factional pressure, face-saving, and personnel control.

## The Chapter Arc

- Ishihara begins the chapter still president in title but not fully master of Nissan; by the end, he has converted formal office into real control.
- Shioji’s private contempt for Ishihara becomes increasingly public, changing the conflict from internal rivalry into open institutional embarrassment.
- Because Japanese labor practice makes a real strike costly and culturally grave, Shioji’s power expresses itself indirectly through stoppages, rhetoric, and pressure rather than a frontal walkout.
- The British factory turns from a foreign investment question into the battlefield on which Ishihara and Shioji test each other’s authority.
- Early opposition inside Nissan makes the British deal look doomed, especially with Kawamata cautious and Shioji actively lobbying against it.
- Kawamata slowly shifts from passive resistance to limited support, changing the balance of power by separating himself from Shioji.
- Ishihara attacks Shioji’s base by taking control of personnel and then peeling away Shioji’s allies inside the union structure.
- Press attention to Shioji’s yacht, finances, and private life changes him from a feared labor power into a public liability.
- The British plant is approved in a reduced, face-saving form, and Shioji’s defeat triggers the Japanese tendency Halberstam emphasizes: once a man is visibly losing, others hurry to align with the winner.

## What To Watch For

- Watch how labor power works at Nissan without becoming a conventional strike. Stoppages, safety claims, union proposals, and public insults all carry political meaning.
- Notice how the British plant is both strategic and symbolic. Strategically, it helps Nissan enter Europe despite quotas; symbolically, it tests whether Japanese management can trust British labor.
- Keep track of Kawamata’s role. He rarely acts like a dramatic protagonist, but his hesitation and later retreat from opposition are decisive.
- Watch the contrast between American and British expansion. The American plant was contentious, but Britain carries a special fear for Japanese executives because of the “British disease” idea.
- Pay attention to the importance of personnel control. Ishihara’s move against Shioji is not just rhetorical; he breaks the union leader’s grip on the personnel department, which had been central to his power.
- The press becomes a weapon. Articles about Shioji’s lifestyle are not gossip in the chapter’s logic; they are part of a campaign to separate him from ordinary workers and national interest.
- Halberstam uses the Nissan fight to puncture the Western image of Japanese companies as frictionless families. The British negotiations expose factional conflict normally hidden from outsiders.
- The final deal’s reduced scale matters. Ishihara wins, but the plant begins as a smaller, more cautious commitment than his original ambition.
- Watch the “winner” dynamic at the end. Shioji does not merely lose an argument; once he is seen as defeated, the social and institutional machinery accelerates his fall.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Shioji insults Ishihara in public.**  
The setup is a culture where subordinates are expected to show deference to superiors, especially in a major corporation. Shioji openly calls Ishihara stupid and suggests Nissan would be stronger under his own leadership. The payoff is that the feud becomes impossible to dismiss as private tension; it is now a public violation of corporate order.

**Work stoppages appear without formal strikes.**  
The setup is Shioji’s need to pressure management without harming workers’ bonuses or breaking Japanese norms around harmony. Assembly lines stop under explanations of safety problems. The payoff is a demonstration that Shioji still has operational power, while also showing the limits on how openly he can use it.

**Shioji goes to the British embassy.**  
The setup is Britain’s intense desire for the Nissan plant and its willingness to offer unusually cooperative labor terms. Shioji attends embassy meetings and attacks British unions and unnamed Nissan executives who want diversification. The payoff is that foreign diplomats see the depth of Nissan’s internal conflict firsthand.

**Kawamata shifts position.**  
The setup is Ishihara’s weak position: Shioji is against the plant, Kawamata is wary, and Nissan is slipping behind Toyota. Pressure from Keidanren and better terms on the British site let Kawamata support a smaller, trial version. The payoff is that Shioji loses his most important cover.

**The Focus magazine article lands.**  
The setup is Ishihara’s effort to expose Shioji’s personal extravagance as a weakness. The magazine links Shioji’s yacht, nightlife, and private life to obstruction of the U.K. deal. The payoff is political, not merely personal: Shioji is framed as holding back Nissan and Japan.

**The announcement almost comes apart.**  
The setup is a fragile consensus requiring speed, ceremony, and face-saving for Kawamata. Nissan calls the British embassy with an urgent plan for a London announcement, then Kawamata becomes unavailable and Ishihara goes instead. The payoff is a public victory for Ishihara, though the deal is smaller than promised.

## Cast And Terms

**Takashi Ishihara**: Nissan president, later chairman; advocate of international expansion and the British plant.

**Ichiro Shioji**: Powerful Nissan union leader; once dominant inside the company, now fighting Ishihara and opposing the U.K. project.

**Katsuji Kawamata**: Nissan chairman; cautious elder figure whose support or opposition shapes whether Ishihara can move.

**Marvin Runyon**: Manager associated with Nissan’s Tennessee plant and its anti-UAW stance.

**Haruki Shimizu**: Shioji deputy whom Ishihara succeeds in turning against him.

**Margaret Thatcher**: British prime minister; wants the Nissan factory as an industrial and political victory.

**Nakasone**: Japanese prime minister criticized by Shioji for appearing to support the British factory.

**Keidanren**: Japan’s powerful federation of business leaders; its pressure helps move Kawamata toward Ishihara.

**Common Market**: The European market Nissan wants to enter from inside, avoiding export limits and anti-Japanese restrictions.

**British disease**: Japanese shorthand for Britain’s perceived industrial decline, labor conflict, weak management, and social decay.

**Focus**: Popular Japanese weekly magazine whose article on Shioji’s yacht and private life becomes a weapon in the struggle.

**Smyrna**: Nissan’s Tennessee plant, originally a truck plant, later confirmed to produce cars as well.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The yacht insults and nautical jokes are vivid, but their importance is mainly that they show the feud becoming personal and public.
- Exact production numbers for the British plant matter less than the direction: Ishihara’s large ambition is scaled down into a cautious first step.
- Shioji’s old resentments toward British union leaders add color, but the central issue is his power struggle with Ishihara.
- Embassy anecdotes matter because they reveal uncertainty and factional politics, not because every diplomatic detail changes the outcome.
