## 1. Orientation Before Reading

- Ohno is explaining how Toyota made just-in-time practical inside a growing organization, not just as a slogan but as a daily operating system.
- The central conflict is between rigid central planning and local, timely adjustment on the factory floor.
- Toyota Motor Company depends on Toyota Automobile Sales Company for real market orders, and on outside cooperating firms for parts and processes.
- The production control department matters, but Ohno argues it cannot act like a brain issuing every small command.
- Kanban becomes the mechanism that lets workers and processes respond to actual use instead of forecast-heavy schedules.
- The stakes are survival in low-growth, volatile markets: excess inventory, excess equipment, and excess workers can consume thin profits.

## 2. The Chapter Arc

- Ohno begins with the body analogy, shifting the image of a company from a command hierarchy to a system with reflexes.
- Planning changes from something to be obeyed rigidly into something that must bend without breaking.
- Information changes from “more and faster is better” into a production input that must arrive only when needed.
- Toyota’s scheduling system narrows detailed sequence information to final assembly, letting kanban pull instructions backward through the plant.
- Fine adjustment changes from a management exception into a built-in capability of the production system.
- Economy changes from accounting comparison or machine purchase logic into cost reduction through manpower reduction and waste elimination.
- Waste becomes visible as a chain reaction: inventory creates warehouses, handling, repairs, records, computers, and finally more mistaken investment.
- Efficiency is redefined: producing more is not improvement unless required demand justifies it.
- The chapter closes by tying Toyota-style industrial engineering to profit, slow-growth survival, and the need for cooperating firms to participate.

## 3. What To Watch For

- The “autonomic nervous system” is Ohno’s way of describing decentralized judgment: workers and processes must know when to stop, start, sequence, and adjust.
- The production plan is treated like a spine: it must be strong enough to support the business and flexible enough to respond to reality.
- Ohno distrusts excess information for the same reason he distrusts excess inventory: both arrive early, pile up, confuse action, and induce waste.
- Kanban is not merely a card system; it is a timing and authority system that turns consumption into a production order.
- Final assembly carries the most detailed sequence information, while earlier processes learn what to do through what has actually been used.
- “Fine adjustment” matters because many small corrections prevent later violent corrections, such as large inventory adjustments or production cuts.
- Ohno’s cost logic repeatedly rejects local optimization: a cheaper outside quote, faster machine, or automated device may still raise total cost.
- Overproduction is the worst waste because it hides waiting, unnecessary motion, excess inventory, and false signs of productivity.
- Required numbers govern everything: tact time, worker count, machine operation, and the meaning of efficiency.

## 4. Load-Bearing Scenes

**The flexible backbone.**  
Setup: A company makes plans through production control.  
Action: Ohno compares rigid adherence to a plan with putting the body’s spine in a cast.  
Payoff: The reader should see that Toyota does not reject planning; it rejects plans that cannot bend.

**Car #20 versus Car #6.**  
Setup: In final assembly, one car has just entered while another is farther down the line.  
Action: Ohno points out that a subprocess installing bumpers may need information about Car #6, not the newest information about Car #20.  
Payoff: This scene makes his information theory concrete: early information can be wrong-timed information.

**The $500 control device.**  
Setup: Someone proposes buying a device to replace one worker.  
Action: Closer study shows the same manpower reduction might be achieved at no cost by changing work sequence.  
Payoff: Ohno’s “economy” is not anti-technology; it is anti-spending before understanding the work.

**The inventory spiral.**  
Setup: A plant has more inventory than it can store.  
Action: It builds a warehouse, hires handlers, buys carts, prevents rust, inventories stock, repairs damage, and maybe buys computers.  
Payoff: Waste multiplies into secondary waste, and the original mistake can disappear inside normal-looking management activity.

**The tempering shop visit.**  
Setup: A supplier says it has enough capacity for Toyota to produce 100,000 cars when Toyota is making about 70,000.  
Action: Ohno asks whether the plant is closed ten days a month; instead, he finds upstream workers racing to keep furnaces full.  
Payoff: Excess capacity has turned into overproduction, inventory, and likely warehouse construction.

## 5. Cast And Terms

**Taiichi Ohno**: Toyota executive and author; explains the production system through plant-level mechanisms and management judgment.

**Toyota Motor Company**: The manufacturing organization whose production system Ohno is describing.

**Toyota Automobile Sales Company**: Sales organization whose dealer orders become the basis for production requirements.

**Cooperating firms**: Outside suppliers and partner companies; Toyota needs them to adopt compatible production practices.

**Production control department**: The planning center; important but, in Ohno’s view, unable to command every small adjustment.

**Just-in-time**: Producing and delivering only what is needed, when needed, in the needed quantity.

**Kanban**: A card or signal that carries production and withdrawal information; in this chapter, it functions as a local production order.

**Autonomation**: Automation with human intelligence; machines or processes stop when abnormality or defect risk appears.

**Production leveling**: Smoothing production quantities and mix so daily work can follow demand without disruptive swings.

**Tact time**: Available production time divided by required daily quantity; the pace implied by actual demand.

**Operating rate**: How much a machine is actually run.

**Operable rate**: Whether a machine is ready to run when needed; Ohno values this more than constant operation.

**Overproduction**: Making more than required or earlier than required; Ohno calls it the worst waste because it hides other wastes.

**Value-added work**: Work that changes the product’s shape, character, or assembly state in a way that advances completion.

## 6. What To Hold Lightly

- The body analogies are vivid, but the operational claim matters more than the metaphor: local reflex beats constant central command.
- The acrobat and aging-spine examples mainly reinforce flexibility; they are not separate production principles.
- The tortoise-and-hare story is memorable, but the core point is continuity paced by demand.
- The discussion of ninjutsu and Japanese characters supports Ohno’s emphasis on trained practice over talk.
- Some comments on controlled economies and free enterprise frame Ohno’s worldview, but the practical mechanism remains fine adjustment under changing demand.
