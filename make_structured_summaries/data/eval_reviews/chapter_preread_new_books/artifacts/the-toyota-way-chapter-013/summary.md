## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter shifts from describing Toyota’s system to asking how another company can actually become lean without reducing the Toyota Way to shop-floor tools.
- The central conflict is between tool adoption and culture change: Six Sigma belts, kaizen events, kanban, 5S, and value stream maps can create gains, but they do not by themselves create a learning enterprise.
- Liker’s standard of success is high. A company must change leadership behavior, daily management, incentives, metrics, problem solving, and the organization of work around value streams.
- Toyota is treated less as a kit to copy than as evidence that culture can be deliberately developed over decades through mentorship, work design, and repeated problem solving.
- The chapter’s examples move from failed or partial transformations to stronger ones: a Six Sigma program that creates internal rivalry, Toyota’s slow globalization, Ford’s Cuautitlan turnaround, and Delphi’s Adrian plant.
- The stakes are survival as much as efficiency. Plants under threat of closure use lean not as a cost program but as a way to win work, improve quality, and rebuild credibility.

## The Chapter Arc

- The chapter opens by narrowing the problem: most companies do not lack lean tools; they lack leaders who understand lean deeply enough to change the old batch-and-queue culture.
- Six Sigma and Lean Sigma are introduced as useful but insufficient improvement programs; the frame changes from “Which tool kit is better?” to “What kind of organization do these tools produce?”
- The automotive supplier example turns a technical program into an organizational warning: young black belts with incentives enter old plants and create resentment instead of shared learning.
- Liker then broadens the claim: TPS is not a set of visible practices but a management philosophy, and companies mistake the visible layer for the underlying system.
- The culture discussion moves below the surface. Kanban, clean floors, charts, teams, and suggestion rates are treated as symptoms of deeper assumptions about problems, learning, and leadership.
- Toyota’s globalization becomes the proof case: exporting the Toyota Way required Japanese coordinators, trips to Japan, technical systems that shape behavior, and senior leaders who model the culture daily.
- Ford’s Cuautitlan plant shows the difference between isolated kaizen gains and enforced cultural change. Early workshops improve work, but only leadership pressure, training, policy deployment, and daily accountability make the system stick.
- The “13 tips” convert the argument into a playbook: start with action, build model value streams, use mapping and kaizen carefully, reorganize around value streams, change metrics, develop leaders, and use experts without outsourcing responsibility.
- Delphi’s Adrian plant closes the chapter as the concrete demonstration: lean tools, value-stream organization, heijunka, visual metrics, and union/workforce alignment turn a threatened plant into a profitable one that wins new business.

## What To Watch For

- Watch the distinction between improving processes and transforming management. Liker accepts tools but keeps asking whether they change daily behavior.
- Notice how incentives can corrupt improvement work. The Six Sigma recruits are rewarded for savings, but the people with process knowledge have little reason to help.
- Track the iceberg metaphor. The visible practices are easy to tour and copy; the hidden assumptions are what make them work.
- Pay attention to time scale. Convis says deep Toyota Way understanding takes closer to ten years than a few weeks of training.
- Watch how technical systems become cultural devices. Flow, pull, and visual controls expose problems and force people to solve them differently.
- Notice Liker’s preference for “go see” models over abstract rollout. A working value stream pilot is meant to teach executives and employees what lean looks like in context.
- Follow the role of management consequences. Cuautitlan changes when lean becomes mandatory, measured, and tied to whether managers keep their jobs.
- Watch for the metric shift from department efficiency to value-stream performance: lead time, inventory, first-time quality, cycle time, and responsiveness matter more than keeping each department locally busy.
- Keep an eye on adaptation. Toyota does not simply transplant Japanese practice unchanged; it modifies work hours, bonuses, and hansei practices in North America.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Six Sigma recruits enter rust-belt plants.**  
Setup: A CEO wants GE-style Six Sigma results and recruits high-GPA young black belts with bonuses and cars. Action: The recruits enter experienced factories with little manufacturing background and need help from people who resent the incentives. Payoff: The program creates rivalry between Six Sigma and lean rather than shared improvement, showing how tool programs can damage culture.

**Toyota tries to globalize its culture.**  
Setup: In the 1980s, Toyota expands abroad and discovers that copying capacity is easier than reproducing Toyota DNA. Action: It assigns Japanese coordinators, sends people to Japan, installs TPS systems, and places senior leaders in overseas operations. Payoff: Culture transfer appears as costly, slow mentorship, not a classroom rollout.

**Cuautitlan nearly dies, then becomes a model.**  
Setup: Ford’s Mexico City-area plant has early kaizen gains but weak senior follow-through, then faces closure rumors. Action: New leadership makes FPS mandatory, trains and tests managers, uses hoshin, and monitors problems daily. Payoff: The plant becomes a Ford Production System showcase and receives new product work.

**Adrian tears down the overhead conveyor.**  
Setup: Delphi’s Adrian plant has hidden inventory circulating overhead and is threatened by low-cost competitors. Action: The plant removes the conveyor, reorganizes into product-family cells, implements kanban, mistake-proofing, and andon. Payoff: Problems become visible, maintenance labor is redeployed, material flows better, and costs fall.

**The heijunka box changes scheduling.**  
Setup: Adrian still suffers from batch scheduling and erratic customer demand even after moving toward flow. Action: A heijunka box visually levels daily production, with cards pulled every 26 minutes to trigger replenishment. Payoff: The plant can smooth mix and volume, reduce chaos, and support frequent color changes.

## Cast And Terms

- **Jeffrey K. Liker**: Author and guide through the chapter’s examples; argues that lean must become a management system.
- **Gary Convis**: Toyota leader used as evidence that deep Toyota Way understanding takes years of immersion.
- **Six Sigma**: Quality-improvement method focused on defect reduction, statistical tools, and belt certification.
- **Lean Sigma**: Hybrid of lean and Six Sigma; Liker treats it skeptically when used as another tool bundle.
- **TPS**: Toyota Production System; in this chapter, the visible technical system and the deeper management philosophy.
- **Edgar Schein**: Culture theorist whose definition helps Liker describe Toyota culture as shared basic assumptions.
- **Genchi genbutsu**: Go and see directly; a Toyota habit of understanding problems at the actual place.
- **Kaizen**: Continuous improvement through practical problem solving.
- **Hansei**: Reflection, often self-critical, used to learn from gaps and failures.
- **Nemawashi**: Consensus-building before decisions.
- **Hoshin / policy deployment**: Aligning objectives, measures, and action plans through the organization.
- **Art Byrne / Wiremold**: Example of CEO-led lean transformation through direct kaizen action.
- **Keith Allman / Merillat**: Example of staged lean rollout with teaching, model lines, and management objectives.
- **Ford Cuautitlan**: Mexican assembly plant that becomes a case of enforced cultural transformation.
- **Delphi Adrian plant**: Former GM parts plant that uses the Delphi Manufacturing System to become profitable and win work.
- **J.T. Battenberg**: Delphi president who backs the Delphi Manufacturing System.
- **John Shook**: Former Toyota manager and lean expert who assists Delphi.
- **Mike Schornack**: Adrian leader quoted on winning GM business through lean transformation.
- **UAW**: United Auto Workers union; part of Delphi’s inherited labor-cost context.
- **Heijunka**: Leveled production scheduling to smooth volume and mix.
- **Andon**: Visual/help signal system that lets operators call attention to problems.
- **5S**: Workplace organization method: sort, set in order, shine, standardize, sustain.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The exact Six Sigma defect-rate footnote matters less than the organizational critique of certification-driven improvement.
- The specific bonus and car incentives in the supplier story are vivid, but the deeper point is misaligned incentives.
- The detailed list of Toyota Technical Center adaptations is secondary to the broader claim that culture must be localized.
- The precise mechanics of Adrian’s heijunka box are less important than why leveling changed behavior.
- The bibliography and index material after the chapter can be ignored for the pre-read unless you want source trails.
