## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in the mid-to-late 1970s, after the first oil shock and before the second. Ford is deciding what cars will reach showrooms around 1978-1979, when the market may be much different.
- Hal Sperlich and Lee Iacocca are pushing for a genuinely small, front-wheel-drive car. Henry Ford II prefers a safer, familiar partial downsizing of existing larger cars.
- The immediate product fight is Panther versus Tiger/Fiesta: Panther means a costly lightening of the old Detroit formula; Tiger/Fiesta means betting earlier and harder on small cars.
- The deeper fight is over who defines Ford’s future: product people reading the market, finance people guarding margins and credit ratings, or Henry Ford II acting as owner of a family company.
- Iacocca is still president, but his real power is draining away. Sperlich’s dependence on him makes Sperlich vulnerable too.
- The chapter turns a car-program argument into a succession struggle. By the end, the product decision, Sperlich’s firing, and Iacocca’s fall all become parts of the same story.

## The Chapter Arc

- Sperlich begins as an advocate for downsizing and becomes a furious prophet, convinced that Ford is refusing to see the market change coming.
- Henry Ford II rejects the idea of a new small front-wheel-drive car and favors Panther, shifting the debate from market adaptation to financial preservation.
- Sperlich keeps answering objections, but the objections keep changing; the fight becomes less about evidence than about institutional resistance.
- Iacocca tries to solve the cost and technology problem by proposing a Ford Fiesta with Honda engine and transmission, briefly making the small-car plan seem practical and fast.
- Henry Ford kills the Honda idea on nationalist and personal grounds, convincing Iacocca and Sperlich that rational counterarguments will not win.
- As the Panther-Tiger showdown nears, Sperlich’s allies disappear, Bidwell defects, and the debate becomes a ritual confirmation of Henry Ford’s decision.
- Ford silences Sperlich in the meeting, Panther wins, and the company commits money to a car that will arrive just as gas prices rise again.
- Sperlich is fired in a blunt, punitive way, proving that Iacocca can no longer protect even his most important lieutenant.
- Iacocca’s authority is then dismantled through McKinsey, Caldwell’s rise, social humiliations, and finally his failed appeal to the board, ending in his own firing.

## What To Watch For

- Product lead time matters. The car being debated in 1974-1976 will hit the market years later, so the real question is not what Americans want now but what they will want after another shock.
- “Small cars mean small profits” is the repeated internal argument. Sperlich’s counter is that high margins per car do not save a company if the market moves elsewhere.
- Henry Ford’s risk calculation is not merely managerial. He sees debt, capital spending, and stock value through the lens of family ownership.
- Iacocca’s formal title lags behind his actual power. He is president, but people begin reading Henry Ford’s displeasure and adjusting before any official change.
- Corporate silence is a signal. Unreturned calls, missing allies, and last-minute defections show how executives sense where power has moved.
- National pride and personal identity override business logic in the Honda episode. The Honda parts offer addresses cost, timing, and technology, but Henry Ford rejects it anyway.
- The board’s sympathy for Iacocca is not the same as willingness to challenge the Ford family. Halberstam makes that distinction crucial.
- Humiliation becomes an instrument of succession: Caldwell’s table placement, reporting lines, airplane permissions, and office notes all announce Iacocca’s decline before the final firing.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Iacocca visits Soichiro Honda.**  
Setup: Ford needs a cheap, quick way to build a small front-wheel-drive car. Action: Iacocca secretly visits Honda, offers a white Mustang, and proposes using Honda engine and transmission parts in the Fiesta. Payoff: the plan seems technically and financially unbeatable until Henry Ford rejects it with a single sentence, revealing that the obstacle is not just cost.

**The final Panther-Tiger meeting.**  
Setup: Sperlich enters believing he can still argue product facts, though the decision is effectively made. Action: he presses the case relentlessly until Henry Ford erupts and tells him to shut up. Payoff: the product debate ends as an assertion of authority, not a weighing of evidence.

**Bidwell’s “Super Bowl” joke.**  
Setup: Ben Bidwell has withdrawn support from Iacocca and Sperlich just before the showdown. Action: at lunch, he jokes that the Panthers beat the Tigers. Payoff: the joke lands badly because it compresses betrayal, defeat, and corporate opportunism into one line.

**Henry Ford explains Erica and Monica.**  
Setup: In a later but related decision, Ford faces another front-wheel-drive investment choice. Action: he tells Bidwell he will approve the smaller Erica/Escort but not the medium-sized Monica because the debt is too much for the family company. Payoff: the chapter gives Henry’s own logic, making him more than a caricature of stubbornness.

**Sperlich is fired.**  
Setup: Sperlich has offended Henry Ford personally and can no longer hide behind Iacocca. Action: Ford orders Iacocca to fire him by day’s end, threatening Iacocca too if he refuses. Payoff: Sperlich’s dismissal publicly exposes Iacocca’s impotence.

**Iacocca goes to the board.**  
Setup: Caldwell has been elevated above him, and Iacocca thinks friendly directors may back him. Action: he takes the company plane to lobby board members. Payoff: Henry Ford treats the move as disloyalty, forces a choice, and the board folds.

## Cast And Terms

- **Hal Sperlich**: Ford product executive, Iacocca ally, leading advocate for the small front-wheel-drive car.
- **Lee Iacocca**: President of Ford, powerful product and sales executive whose authority collapses through the chapter.
- **Henry Ford II**: Chairman, Ford family heir, ultimate owner-figure inside the company.
- **Panther**: Henry Ford’s favored program, a partially downsized version of an existing middle-level Ford car.
- **Tiger**: The alternative modest downsizing plan paired with leaving enough money for a smaller car.
- **Fiesta**: The small car Iacocca and Sperlich want Ford to pursue more seriously.
- **Honda / Soichiro Honda**: Japanese automaker and founder; potential supplier of engine and transmission parts for the Fiesta.
- **Ben Bidwell**: Sales group vice-president for North America, respected product man whose withdrawal from Tiger matters.
- **Bill Bourke**: Head of North America, Iacocca ally and messenger in several painful moments.
- **Phil Caldwell**: Executive elevated above Iacocca after the McKinsey study.
- **McKinsey**: Outside consulting firm whose study helps formalize Iacocca’s decline.
- **Erica / Escort**: Later small front-wheel-drive program that Henry reluctantly approves.
- **Monica**: Later medium-sized front-wheel-drive program Henry refuses because of cost.
- **Ford board**: Outside directors and family members who may sympathize with Iacocca but ultimately will not overrule Henry Ford II.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The fireworks, vase, and Mustang presentation in Japan are vivid color; the important point is the Honda deal’s practicality and Henry Ford’s rejection.
- The High Noon comparison mainly captures Sperlich’s loneliness before the meeting.
- Bidwell’s Panther-Tiger football joke is memorable, but its value is as a sign of power realignment.
- Iacocca’s social scorekeeping around birthdays, college football, and neighbors matters less than the broader erosion of his authority.
