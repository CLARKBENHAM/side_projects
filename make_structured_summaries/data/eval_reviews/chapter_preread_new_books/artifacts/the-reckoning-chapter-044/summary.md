## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter follows Joel Goddard after his long layoff from Ford and uses his return to work as a way to measure how industrial security in America has changed.
- The immediate setting moves from a small nonunion machine shop in Michigan to Ford’s Rawsonville plant, where Goddard had once assumed union wages and steady work were normal.
- The central conflict is not simply worker versus boss, or Ford versus Japan. It is Goddard versus his old assumptions about skill, loyalty, unions, corporations, and permanence.
- The broader auto industry is temporarily recovering because import restrictions have limited Japanese competition, but the recovery is fragile and partly artificial.
- Rawsonville is vulnerable because it makes parts and dies, exactly the kind of work Japanese suppliers, Korean competitors, Mexican plants, and automation threaten.
- Ford and the UAW try to create a compromise called Permanent Employment Guarantee, trading wage growth and rigid job classifications for job security.
- Goddard becomes an unlikely messenger for change: a once-casual union man who has been scared enough by unemployment to recognize that the old bargain may be gone.

## The Chapter Arc

- Goddard begins grateful for a job at a nonunion machine shop, but that gratitude turns into anger as he learns what work feels like without union protection or leverage.
- The nonunion shop changes his understanding of power: wages, praise, bathroom breaks, raises, and even the timing of paychecks all depend on the boss’s arbitrary control.
- When Ford calls him back to Rawsonville in March 1982, his return feels like rescue, but it no longer restores his old innocence about work.
- Back at Ford, he sees that many laid-off workers have not recovered; the layoff was not a temporary inconvenience but a social and family rupture.
- Ford’s recovery initially looks encouraging, but Goddard now reads every sign through fear: foreign suppliers, automation, Mexican production, and plant closings all point toward his own replaceability.
- His coworkers’ indifference to company warnings reveals a split inside the plant between those who have suffered the new economy and those still protected by seniority or denial.
- The PEG proposal forces workers to choose between immediate wage gains and future security; the first vote fails because distrust of Ford and the union is deeper than management expected.
- On the second attempt, Goddard helps sell the agreement by personal testimony, and the plant accepts security in exchange for concessions.
- The chapter ends by pushing the fear into the next generation: when Goddard’s son says he can always do his father’s work, Goddard tells him those jobs will not exist.

## What To Watch For

- Watch how Halberstam contrasts union and nonunion work through atmosphere rather than abstract ideology: chairs, bathroom time, praise, raises, fear, and humiliation.
- Notice that Goddard’s transformation comes less from reading economics than from being unemployed and then working under a petty autocrat.
- The chapter treats gratitude as politically unstable. Goddard is grateful to have work, but gratitude cannot erase arbitrary power or bad treatment.
- Rawsonville’s danger comes from several directions at once: Japanese quality and cost, Korean rise, Mexican labor costs, automation, and Ford’s own diversification.
- The union is still important, but its power is narrowed. Global competition gives management a standing argument for concessions.
- The PEG agreement is a compromise born from fear, not triumph. It protects jobs temporarily while giving Ford more flexibility and freezing wages.
- Ford’s purchase of Japanese diemaking machines matters because it exposes a gap between nationalist rhetoric and corporate purchasing logic.
- Goddard’s thinking becomes more individualistic without becoming anti-union. He values the union more, but he also concludes that institutions will protect themselves first.
- The final father-son exchange is the chapter’s sharpest consequence: the loss of industrial certainty becomes a family inheritance.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**The nonunion shop’s daily discipline.**  
Setup: Goddard has been out of work for more than two years and is grateful for any decent job. Action: He discovers a shop where the boss controls bathroom breaks, watches output, withholds praise, and makes raises depend on personal submission. Payoff: The reader sees what union protection had hidden from him: without collective leverage, skill alone does not guarantee dignity.

**Goddard quits when Ford calls him back.**  
Setup: Ford recalls him to Rawsonville in March 1982. Action: He tells the shop owner he appreciates the job but that the treatment was unfair, then leaves with blunt contempt. Payoff: His anger clarifies the chapter’s moral baseline. Work is not only wages; it is also respect, predictability, and limits on arbitrary authority.

**The slide show about Japanese competition.**  
Setup: Ford tries to warn workers that competitors like Nippondenso threaten Rawsonville. Action: Goddard absorbs the warning with dread, while another worker dismisses it because he is being paid to sit there. Payoff: Goddard recognizes his former self in the complacent worker and understands that layoff experience has changed his political and economic imagination.

**The first PEG vote fails.**  
Setup: Ford and the UAW expect workers to accept a job-security plan in exchange for wage freezes and looser classifications. Action: Workers reject it almost two to one. Payoff: The vote reveals that even a valuable concession can fail if workers distrust both management and union leadership.

**The second PEG campaign succeeds.**  
Setup: The UAW lobbies harder, and Goddard becomes an advocate. Action: He tells coworkers that Rawsonville is exposed to the new world economy and that they are endangered. Ford’s promise of $10 million in new machinery helps persuade them. Payoff: The agreement passes, showing fear can move workers toward compromise when abstract warnings become personal.

**Scott Goddard’s future closes off.**  
Setup: Goddard’s teenage son considers what he might do if college does not work out. Action: Scott says he can always do what his father does. Payoff: Goddard answers that he cannot, because those jobs are disappearing. The chapter’s economic argument lands as a parental warning.

## Cast And Terms

**Joel Goddard**: Ford diemaking worker at Rawsonville; laid off, rehired at a nonunion shop, then recalled to Ford. The chapter’s central consciousness.

**Joyce Goddard**: Joel’s wife; her steadiness and earning ability help the family survive the layoff years.

**Scott / Scotty Goddard**: Joel’s seventeen-year-old son, whose future makes the decline of industrial work feel immediate.

**Rawsonville**: Ford plant in Michigan where Goddard works; a parts and diemaking facility vulnerable to foreign suppliers and internal Ford cost decisions.

**UAW**: United Auto Workers, the union representing Ford workers. Still powerful, but increasingly constrained by global competition.

**PEG, Permanent Employment Guarantee**: Rawsonville pilot agreement promising job protection during the contract in exchange for wage freezes and more flexible job classifications.

**Nippondenso**: Major Japanese auto supplier and competitor, newly present in Michigan and capable of serving both Japanese automakers and the Big Three.

**Mazda**: Japanese automaker connected to Ford; a symbol here of Ford’s possible shift away from relying on plants like Rawsonville.

**Lee Iacocca**: Chrysler leader whose praise of buying cheaper outside parts alarms Goddard because it points against Ford’s vertically integrated labor model.

**Voluntary restrictions**: Limits on Japanese car imports that temporarily helped American automakers recover before being relaxed in late 1985.

**Diemaking**: Skilled manufacturing work producing dies used in auto production; valuable but threatened by automation and cheaper foreign production.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The exact insults and shop-floor remarks are vivid, but the larger point is the power imbalance in nonunion work.
- The bathroom and paycheck details matter as examples, not as isolated grievances.
- The Japanese machines’ quality matters less than what their purchase reveals about Ford’s incentives.
- The exact vote margins are less important than the reversal from suspicion to fearful acceptance.
- Goddard’s renewed confidence near the end should be held alongside his continuing unease; the chapter does not present his security as settled.
