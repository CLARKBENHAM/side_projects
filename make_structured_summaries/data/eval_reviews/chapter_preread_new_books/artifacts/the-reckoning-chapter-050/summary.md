## Orientation Before Reading

- By the mid-1980s, Japan’s victory in automobiles has become part of a larger Western anxiety: Japan is no longer merely a fast-rising manufacturer but a financial and industrial power whose trade surplus gives it political weight.
- The central tension is between Japan’s economic strength and its unwillingness, in Halberstam’s view, to behave like a responsible international power: open markets, reciprocal trade, and a broader sense of obligation.
- The chapter is less about factories than about attitude: Japan’s protectionism, insularity, self-mythology, insecurity, and resentment of Western scrutiny.
- American criticism here comes not mainly from anti-Japanese hardliners, but from longtime friends of Japan such as Edwin Reischauer and Henry Rosovsky, which makes the critique harder to dismiss.
- The tobacco dispute functions as the cleanest case study: American cigarettes are cheaper and better, but Japan’s official and unofficial systems keep them out anyway.
- Halberstam frames Japan as both arrogant and anxious: proud of its industrial triumph, yet fearful that Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, and other lower-cost Asian competitors may someday do to Japan what Japan did to America.

## The Chapter Arc

- Japan’s postwar success changes from an admired achievement into a source of resentment because trade has become dramatically one-sided: the West ships raw materials in and buys finished Japanese goods back.
- The diplomatic mood shifts when even Japan’s most sympathetic American friends lose patience with Japanese delay tactics on opening markets.
- Edwin Reischauer’s private frustration becomes a warning sign: if someone so committed to Japan is angry, the trade imbalance has moved from technical dispute to political danger.
- Henry Rosovsky broadens the issue from cars and consumer goods to future industries, arguing that Japanese protectionism threatens American high technology, biotechnology, and venture-backed innovation.
- The chapter narrows to tobacco, where Japan’s market-opening promises prove hollow because control over distribution and retail behavior matters more than formal policy announcements.
- The Japan Tobacco Inc. scandal changes the reader’s sense of Japanese protectionism from abstract unfairness to concrete manipulation: posters torn down, foreign cigarettes hidden, supply restricted, and trademarks preemptively seized.
- Halberstam then widens the lens from trade practice to culture, portraying Japan as technologically modern but socially guarded, borrowing Western tools while resisting Western influence.
- Japan’s confidence turns into self-mythology: the country begins to see its own way of doing things not only as successful but as superior.
- The final movement complicates the portrait: beneath the arrogance lies fragility, fear of softness, fear of decline, and fear that cheaper, hungrier Asian competitors will overtake Japan.

## What To Watch For

- Watch how Halberstam distinguishes legal openness from practical openness. A market can be officially “opened” while distribution channels, trademarks, ministry pressure, and retailer behavior keep foreigners out.
- Notice the importance of distributorships. In the tobacco example, foreign brands depend on Japan Tobacco Inc., the very domestic competitor with every incentive to limit them.
- Track the role of “slow-walking.” Halberstam argues that Japan moves quickly when change serves its interests, but invokes patience, procedure, and cultural difference when change threatens protected sectors.
- Pay attention to who is criticizing Japan. Reischauer and Rosovsky matter because they are not casual antagonists; they are establishment intellectuals with long-standing sympathy for Japan.
- Watch the shift from economic complaint to moral indictment. The chapter moves from trade imbalance to claims about Japan’s “soul,” insularity, and unwillingness to accept reciprocity.
- Keep an eye on the contrast between surface prosperity and household reality: expensive suits, company perks, long commutes, small apartments, and limited personal wealth.
- Note the “powerful and weak” contradiction. Japan wants recognition for its strength but also claims vulnerability whenever outsiders ask it to accept the duties of strength.
- Automation is not just a factory issue here. It is Japan’s strategic answer to rising wages and the threat of lower-cost Asian producers.
- The final Drucker anecdote compresses the chapter’s economic logic: if Japan cannot automate enough, production may move to cheaper countries, and Japanese workers may lose the very advantage they built.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Reischauer with the Japanese editors.**  
Setup: Edwin Reischauer, one of America’s most committed friends of Japan, meets fifteen visiting Japanese editors. Action: he warns them that the trade imbalance has become politically explosive, but they respond with silence or indifference. Payoff: the scene shows that the crisis is serious enough to alienate Japan’s defenders, not just its critics.

**Rosovsky’s warning on high technology.**  
Setup: Henry Rosovsky writes to the New York Times about Japanese protectionism in fields tied to America’s future economic hopes. Action: he argues that Japan blocks foreign products until domestic firms can match them. Payoff: protectionism is presented not as a nuisance but as a mechanism that denies American innovators the profits needed to fund the next cycle of invention.

**The tobacco market opening that barely opens.**  
Setup: American cigarettes are cheaper and better than Japanese cigarettes, and Japan promises liberalization under U.S. pressure. Action: Japan Tobacco Inc. uses control of distribution and retail pressure to keep foreign brands hidden, scarce, or disadvantaged. Payoff: the episode demonstrates how Japan can preserve protectionism without needing an explicit law that says “foreigners may not compete.”

**The trademark maneuver.**  
Setup: Foreign cigarette companies prepare to introduce additional brands into Japan. Action: the head of Japan Tobacco Inc. quietly applies for Japanese trademark rights to roughly fifty foreign brands. Payoff: the act crystallizes Western disgust because it looks less like defensive nationalism and more like predatory obstruction by a country already enjoying a huge trade surplus.

**The Drucker factory exchange.**  
Setup: Peter Drucker tours a Japanese auto plant and hears a manager explain how hard it is to push automation beyond the first stages. Action: Drucker asks what happens if the plant fails to automate enough. Payoff: the labor leader’s answer, that the manager’s successor will run a plant in the Philippines, reveals Japan’s fear of being undercut by the same low-cost logic it once used against America.

## Cast And Terms

**Edwin Reischauer**: Harvard Japanese studies scholar and former U.S. ambassador to Japan; an important pro-Japan voice whose frustration signals a turning point.

**Henry Rosovsky**: Former Harvard dean who warns publicly about Japanese protectionism in advanced industries.

**Frank Gibney**: Writer cited for a Japanese intellectual’s image of Japan treating the world like an animal park viewed from inside a car.

**Prime Minister Yasuhiro Nakasone**: Japanese prime minister who meets with Ronald Reagan and announces market-opening efforts that, in Halberstam’s telling, produce little practical change.

**President Ronald Reagan**: U.S. president involved in summit diplomacy with Japan during the trade disputes.

**Japan Tobacco Inc. / JTI**: Government-owned tobacco company that controls domestic distribution and becomes the chapter’s clearest example of protectionist practice.

**Philip Morris**: American cigarette company; its Tokyo representative compares the distribution setup to Pepsi being distributed by Coca-Cola.

**Donald Richie**: Longtime Japan resident and writer who calls Japan “the electronic tribe,” a phrase Halberstam uses to capture high technology joined to social traditionalism.

**Herman Kahn**: American futurist whose prediction of a coming Japanese age feeds Japanese self-confidence and self-mythology.

**Peter Drucker**: Business historian whose factory-tour anecdote closes the chapter’s argument about automation and vulnerability.

**Japan Inc.**: Western shorthand for Japan as a coordinated state-business system pursuing national economic advantage.

**Little Orphan Japan Syndrome**: Western observers’ phrase for Japan’s habit of presenting itself as vulnerable and put-upon despite its economic power.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The baseball anecdote about Japanese pitchers walking an American home-run leader is vivid, but it mainly illustrates insularity rather than driving the trade argument.
- Details about Tokyo districts such as Ginza, Roppongi, and Akasaka help set the atmosphere of surface affluence but are not central mechanisms.
- The comment about Parker pens, Cross pencils, and French neckties is useful as a snapshot of complacency, not as a policy argument.
- The worries about young people leaving work on time, buying cars, or girls not tying kimonos are cultural texture around anxiety over softness and decline.
