## 1. Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter centers on Ichiro Shioji, the Nissan union leader who becomes one of the most powerful men inside the company without formally joining management.
- The time frame runs from Shioji’s postwar formation through the 1950s and 1960s, especially the consolidation after the 1953 Nissan strike, the 1965 Prince merger, and the shift to two-shift production.
- The core conflict is not simply labor versus management. It is control versus disorder, with Shioji’s union serving as both worker organization and disciplinary arm of Nissan management.
- Kawamata, Nissan’s president, depends on Shioji because Shioji can deliver the workforce, suppress dissent, and make difficult production decisions politically possible.
- Shioji’s power grows from anti-Communist labor politics, American labor contacts, organizational ruthlessness, and a sharp understanding of Nissan as a political machine.
- The chapter also shifts to Sanosuke Tanaka, an older worker whose loyalty, hardship, pride, and exhaustion show what Nissan’s modernization felt like on the shop floor.

## 2. The Chapter Arc

- Miyake’s claim on a Nissan board seat turns him from useful union ally into a threat to hierarchy, allowing Shioji to replace him as the real union power.
- Shioji’s ambition is defined away from titles and toward command: he discovers that union leadership can give him more direct authority than management rank.
- His outsider status, night-school background, and resentment of Japanese educational snobbery become part of his political identity rather than a source of shame.
- A postwar incident involving a beaten American soldier pushes Shioji away from Communist-led unions because he concludes that ideology can override truth.
- American labor institutions, especially the AFL-CIO and Walter Reuther’s UAW, give Shioji a model of the labor leader as public man, power broker, and disciplined internal boss.
- Inside Nissan, Shioji converts that model into a tightly controlled company union where dissent, wage complaints, and left-wing agitation are treated as political threats.
- Shunichiro Umetani’s challenge at a union meeting exposes the bargain beneath Shioji’s authority: workers are asked to trust Shioji’s personal guarantee instead of written protections.
- The Prince merger proves Shioji’s organizational genius and brutality, as he dismantles a rival union worker by worker and leader by leader.
- The chapter ends by bringing the cost of Nissan’s growth down to the level of bodies, homes, sleep, and families, especially through Tanaka’s experience with relentless production and night shifts.

## 3. What To Watch For

- Watch how Halberstam separates formal authority from actual authority. Shioji is not on the board, but he can make decisions that board members need.
- Notice the paradox of a labor union that strengthens management. Shioji’s union gets benefits for workers, but it also delivers obedience, surveillance, and political silence.
- Keep track of the anti-Communist context. Shioji’s early politics are not abstract ideology; they are formed through street conflict, union factionalism, and the red purge era.
- Watch the American influence carefully. Shioji admires American openness and labor prestige, but he borrows the power and discipline of the UAW more than its democratic surface.
- The Prince merger is a mechanism chapter inside the chapter: Shioji classifies workers, recruits leaders first, uses peer pressure, implies job consequences, and isolates holdouts.
- Tanaka’s loyalty is not presented as stupidity. It is rooted in poverty, wartime loss, home ownership, rising wages, pride in production, and a belief that order rescued Nissan.
- Quality-control circles appear here less as pure technical innovation than as a way to restore community and reduce alienation in a rapidly expanding workforce.
- The move to two shifts shows how a production technique imported from America collides with Japanese housing conditions. Same factory logic, different domestic reality.

## 4. Load-Bearing Scenes

**Miyake asks for the board.**  
Setup: Miyake has helped protect Kawamata and expects a reward. Action: he asks for a Nissan board seat, violating assumptions about rank, timing, and the proper place of a union man. Payoff: his ambition makes him vulnerable, and Shioji replaces him as Kawamata’s indispensable labor ally.

**The American soldier at the rally.**  
Setup: Postwar left-wing rallies are building toward the planned February 1947 general strike. Action: demonstrators beat a confused American soldier as a spy, and Shioji intervenes because he believes the charge is false. Payoff: when union leaders insist the incident can still serve the cause, Shioji turns against Communist union leadership.

**Shioji meets Walter Reuther.**  
Setup: Shioji attends a Harvard seminar through American labor and embassy connections. Action: he sees Reuther draw a huge crowd, meets him, and shares a symbolic handshake despite Reuther’s injured right hand. Payoff: Shioji gains a model of labor leadership as respected, public, forceful, and internally disciplined.

**Umetani asks for guarantees.**  
Setup: Nissan workers are told to accept a reduced wage increase because trade liberalization makes life hard for the company. Action: Umetani asks what the union gets in return and presses for guarantees. Payoff: Shioji answers that his own guarantee is enough, revealing the union’s personalized authority and the room’s fear.

**The Prince union is dismantled.**  
Setup: Nissan merges with Prince, whose union is stronger, more left-wing, and better paid. Action: Shioji’s side classifies every worker, courts leaders, threatens consequences, and pressures workers to bring colleagues over. Payoff: the Prince union collapses almost completely, and Suzuki realizes he has been conquered, not merely defeated.

**Tanaka tries to survive two shifts.**  
Setup: Nissan needs more production from limited facilities. Action: workers alternate day and night shifts, but small homes, noisy children, disrupted meals, and exhaustion make sleep nearly impossible. Payoff: even loyal workers resist, but Shioji accepts the misery as unavoidable in Japan’s industrial conditions.

## 5. Cast And Terms

- **Ichiro Shioji**: Nissan union leader; anti-Communist, ambitious, confrontational, and central to enforcing labor discipline.
- **Miyake**: Shioji’s superior in the union before being pushed aside after seeking a Nissan board seat.
- **Kawamata**: Nissan president; relies on Shioji to control labor and stabilize the company.
- **Masuda union**: Earlier militant Nissan union defeated in the 1953 strike; its memory haunts later labor politics.
- **Shunichiro Umetani**: Young Nissan worker who questions Shioji’s wage concession logic and later leaves the company.
- **Sanosuke Tanaka**: Veteran Nissan worker whose loyalty and suffering anchor the shop-floor view of modernization.
- **Prince**: Auto company merged into Nissan in 1965; its union becomes Shioji’s target.
- **Takashi Suzuki**: Leader of the Prince union, overwhelmed by Shioji’s organizing tactics.
- **MITI**: Ministry of International Trade and Industry; pushes auto-industry rationalization and encourages mergers.
- **Sohyo / Domei**: Labor federations; Sohyo is treated as more left-wing, Domei as more cooperative with management.
- **Walter Reuther / UAW**: American labor model that shapes Shioji’s idea of what a powerful union leader can be.
- **Quality-control circles**: Worker discussion groups used to improve quality and restore some workplace community.
- **Two shifts**: Nissan’s move to run factories longer each day, creating severe strain because Japanese workers’ homes make daytime sleep difficult.

## 6. What To Hold Lightly

- Shioji’s blazer, yacht, Ginza life, and complaints about hypocrisy matter as signals of status rebellion, but they are not the central mechanism.
- The dance-instructor episode is useful color for Shioji’s postwar improvisation, not a major causal hinge.
- The “Johnny Walker Japanese” phrase captures Shioji’s contempt for superficial Westernization, but the deeper point is his selective borrowing from America.
- Tanaka’s shrine charm and accident-free car history are vivid evidence of pride and aspiration; the larger point is his entry into Japan’s new middle class.
