## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter is a portrait of Walter Reuther, the UAW leader who turned personal austerity, socialist conviction, and tactical organizing into one of the most powerful positions in American industrial life.
- The central setting is Detroit auto labor from the Depression through the postwar boom, with Ford, GM, Chrysler, the UAW, the New Deal state, and later Washington all tied into the same power system.
- Halberstam treats Reuther as both antagonist and product of the auto companies: he fought their power, but eventually became part of the industry structure that made high wages, high prices, big cars, and monopoly-style bargaining mutually reinforcing.
- The chapter’s early tension is moral: Reuther sees himself as a restraint on concentrated corporate power, while figures like George Romney worry that the union itself has become another unchecked institution.
- The chapter’s later tension is historical: the conditions that made Reuther effective in the 1930s and 1950s become less stable as imports, foreign wage gaps, and political conflicts like Vietnam expose the limits of his system.
- Watch the family background closely. Halberstam makes Reuther’s organizing style feel almost inherited: German socialism, Lutheran discipline, immigrant austerity, debate training, and a near-religious idea of social justice.

## The Chapter Arc

- Reuther begins as the man who can puncture Detroit’s corporate logic, especially the auto companies’ habit of treating price increases as competitive necessity.
- His public image shifts from ordinary labor leader to exceptional figure: incorruptible, humorless, disciplined, and convinced that he is morally and intellectually superior to most of the executives he bargains against.
- The chapter moves backward into his family formation, showing that Reuther’s politics were not acquired casually; they were built into the household by Val and Anna Reuther through religion, poverty, argument, and socialist grievance.
- Reuther’s early working life changes him from a skilled tool-and-die worker into a professional organizer after he is fired for protest and radicalized by Depression-era Detroit.
- The return from Europe and the Soviet Union changes his status: he comes back to Detroit not merely as a young militant but as a seasoned organizer entering a suddenly more favorable New Deal environment.
- The Kelsey-Hayes sit-down strike changes Local 174 from a tiny unit into a real power base and proves that Reuther can combine theater, timing, discipline, and bureaucracy.
- The Flint sit-down strike changes the whole industry by forcing GM to recognize the UAW; even though Walter’s role is smaller than his brothers’, the victory creates the world in which he can rise.
- Reuther’s 1946 victory for the UAW presidency changes him from local organizer to national institutional power, and assassination attempts deepen his heroic standing among workers.
- By the end, Reuther’s success changes into entanglement: the union wins prosperity, but that prosperity depends on the same big-company system, pricing practices, political bargains, and big-car economics he distrusts.

## What To Watch For

- Reuther’s austerity is not decorative character detail. It helps explain why workers trust him and why rival labor leaders, corporate executives, and congressional investigators have trouble attacking him as corrupt.
- Halberstam repeatedly contrasts moral purity with institutional power. Reuther believes good intentions can redeem the union’s strength; Romney’s doubt hangs over the rest of the chapter.
- The sit-down strike matters as a practical innovation. It turns workers’ physical presence inside plants into leverage because management fears damage to machinery and production paralysis.
- Government neutrality is decisive. Before the New Deal, courts, police, and company guards are aligned with owners; under Roosevelt and Governor Frank Murphy, organizers gain room to act.
- The Reuther brothers function as a political network. Walter’s ambition is amplified by Roy and Victor, who extend his information, local contact, and organizing reach.
- The UAW’s power rests on industry structure. A few huge companies, flush demand, and fear of shutdowns give the union leverage that would not exist in a fragmented or weaker market.
- Higher labor costs do not stay inside the company. Halberstam emphasizes the pass-through to car prices, making workers, companies, and consumers part of the same bargain.
- Reuther’s small-car plan is an early sign that he sees the import threat, but cannot free himself from the system that big wages and big cars have produced.
- Vietnam reveals the cost of insider power: Reuther’s loyalty to Johnson and concern for negotiations collide with the antiwar moral claims of a younger generation.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**The orange-juice squeezer.** Reuther attends Florida labor conventions but refuses their expense-account culture. He brings his own oranges and squeezer rather than pay hotel prices. The payoff is a miniature of his authority: he polices his own appetites so he can police the union’s.

**The Sunday debates in the Reuther home.** Val Reuther turns church sermons and political issues into family disputations, assigning topics like suffrage, pacifism, socialism, and military spending. The payoff is practical training: the Reuther boys learn to argue under pressure long before Detroit labor battles require it.

**Kelsey-Hayes.** Victor is inside the plant, a woman worker pretends to faint, the line stops, and management summons Walter to end the strike. Walter uses the company-provided entrance to make an organizing speech instead. The payoff is explosive growth for Local 174 and proof that Reuther can turn a small opening into institutional power.

**Flint and Chevy Number 4.** Union leaders mislead company spies about the target, draw management toward Chevrolet Plant Number 9, and seize Number 4, which controls Chevy engine production. The payoff is GM’s paralysis and eventual recognition of the UAW.

**The assassination attempts.** Reuther and Victor are attacked by gunmen, with Victor losing an eye. Police do little, and the union concludes anti-union owners used underworld violence. The payoff is martyr-like legitimacy for Reuther and a clearer picture of how bitter the labor struggle remains.

**The 1968 Passover dinner.** Young antiwar dissenters confront Reuther over Vietnam, and he explains that delicate negotiations make a break with Johnson difficult. The payoff is the chapter’s sharpest moral reversal: labor gains and establishment access now constrain the radical outsider.

## Cast And Terms

**Walter Reuther**: President of the United Auto Workers; socialist, organizer, negotiator, and central figure of the chapter.

**Valentine Reuther**: Walter’s father; German socialist, labor agitator, Debs supporter, and political educator of his sons.

**Anna Stocker Reuther**: Walter’s mother; Lutheran, austere, morally demanding, and central to the family’s discipline.

**Victor Reuther**: Walter’s brother and fellow organizer; active at Kelsey-Hayes and later wounded in an assassination attempt.

**Roy Reuther**: Walter’s brother; gifted organizer, important in Flint and in extending Walter’s network.

**UAW**: United Auto Workers, the union Reuther helps build into a major industrial and political force.

**Big Three**: GM, Ford, and Chrysler, the dominant American auto companies.

**Local 174**: Reuther’s west-side Detroit UAW local, transformed by the Kelsey-Hayes strike.

**Kelsey-Hayes**: Ford supplier whose successful sit-down strike becomes Reuther’s breakthrough.

**Flint sit-down strike**: 1936-37 strike against GM that forces recognition of the UAW.

**Frank Murphy**: Michigan governor whose refusal to crush the Flint strikers is crucial.

**Engine Charley Wilson / Big Bill Knudsen**: Auto executives Reuther respects as more decent and substantial than most managers.

**George Romney**: Auto executive and Reuther friend who warns that union power can also become monopoly power.

**Leonard Woodcock / Leslie Woodcock / Irving and Barry Bluestone**: UAW family circle figures in the Vietnam-era confrontation near the chapter’s end.

## What To Hold Lightly

- The jokes about Reuther’s dullness, long speeches, and indifference to the Detroit Tigers are vivid, but mainly reinforce his single-mindedness.
- The chicken-feed episode is memorable family color; its point is Reuther thoroughness, not poultry economics.
- The exact salary and investment anecdotes matter less than the broader claim that Reuther’s personal financial life was unusually restrained.
- The Passover details matter mainly because they stage the Vietnam conflict inside an intimate UAW family setting.
