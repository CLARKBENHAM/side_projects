## 1. Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in Nissan’s labor crisis in spring and summer 1953, when Tetsuo Masuda’s radical union appears strongest but is approaching its decisive vulnerability.
- The central fight is no longer just wages. It is over who controls the factory floor, who defines legitimate authority, and whether postwar labor rights will remake Japanese corporate life or be absorbed by it.
- Masuda’s strength comes from his bond with workers: he gives moral language to resentments they had been trained to bury under hierarchy, deference, and duty.
- Management’s counterforce consolidates around Katsuji Kawamata, the banker-turned-Nissan executive, who sees the union as the last obstacle between Nissan and a larger industrial future.
- Middle management becomes the pivot. Section chiefs and ambitious white-collar employees are pulled out of the old union and become the base for a company-friendly second union.
- The conflict becomes physical and domestic: factory-floor intimidation, lockouts, barricades, dormitory pressure, wives, paychecks, hunger, and fear all become instruments of power.

## 2. The Chapter Arc

- Masuda begins at peak authority: management pressure only deepens his hold on workers, because he speaks as an elite figure validating the grievances of non-elite men.
- The union’s shop-floor resistance shifts from disorder to coercion: late arrivals, meetings, and work stoppages give way to the brutal `suribachi` trials of section chiefs.
- Management’s dilemma changes into Kawamata’s opportunity: Asahara appears frightened and trapped, while Kawamata becomes the man willing to fight the union directly.
- Kawamata adopts the decisive strategy of the era: create or support a second, company-friendly union that can claim legal labor legitimacy while serving management’s aims.
- Masaru Miyake emerges as the organizer of that second union, drawing on middle-management resentment, anti-communism, and fear that Masuda’s union will destroy the company.
- The chapter widens into biography: Miyake’s wartime experience and Kawamata’s austere upbringing explain why both men distrust radicalism, weakness, and disorder.
- Ichiro Shioji enters as a new kind of postwar power figure: classless, ambitious, physically tough, and at home in intimidation and political maneuver.
- The strike beginning May 25 changes the balance of power: both sides want confrontation, but management has money, banks, suppliers, Nikkeiren backing, police tolerance, and time.
- Kawamata’s August lockout and the second union’s public emergence reverse the initiative: Masuda still has crowds, but Miyake and Shioji now offer pay, protection, and a way out.

## 3. What To Watch For

- Watch how Halberstam treats authority as psychological, not only institutional. Masuda succeeds because he gives workers permission to feel what hierarchy taught them to deny.
- The `suribachi` courts matter because they win space while losing legitimacy. They frighten section chiefs into submission, but also harden them emotionally against the old union.
- The second union is the key mechanism. It lets management fight labor through a labor institution, using the legal form created by American reforms against the radical substance those reforms encouraged.
- Money changes time. Kawamata can wait because banks, suppliers, and Nikkeiren support give management endurance; workers without pay cannot wait indefinitely.
- Domestic pressure is part of the battlefield. Wives, dormitories, food money, and family fear become more powerful than slogans once the strike drags on.
- The chapter repeatedly contrasts ideology with livelihood. Masuda thinks in speeches and historical categories; many workers think in paychecks, kitchens, children, and the next day.
- Anti-communism becomes a practical organizing weapon. Miyake and Shioji frame Masuda’s union as alien, foreign, Russian, and anti-Japanese.
- Halberstam is also explaining Japanese capitalism’s postwar settlement: radical labor is not simply defeated by repression, but by loyal unions, banking networks, coordinated employers, and controlled competition.

## 4. Load-Bearing Scenes

**Masuda in the rain.**  
Setup: Workers gather under umbrellas in a heavy drizzle, physically separated from one another and from the speaker. Action: Masuda tells them to put the umbrellas down and come closer. Payoff: The scene shows his gift for turning a crowd into a body and making obedience to him feel like intimacy.

**The `suribachi` trial of a kacho.**  
Setup: Section chiefs have been reclassified as management and cut away from union legitimacy. Action: Workers surround a kacho’s desk, insult and interrogate him for hours, rotating fresh accusers as he weakens. Payoff: The union proves it can dominate the factory floor, but the cruelty helps create the very anti-union force that will later defeat it.

**Miyake at the Ito labor conference.**  
Setup: Miyake is still a minor delegate, uneasy with Masuda’s radicalism but not yet the open leader of a rival union. Action: He challenges Masuda over who will pay for the raises, and Masuda’s allies browbeat him late into the night. Payoff: Miyake reads the old union as a threat not only to jobs but to freedom, and he moves toward organized rebellion.

**Kawamata waits through the strike.**  
Setup: The union expects confrontation to display its strength. Action: Kawamata refuses contact, hides managers, secures loans, protects suppliers, and lets hunger work against the strikers. Payoff: Management stops reacting and begins setting the terms of the conflict.

**Miyake’s public rally.**  
Setup: The second union has only a tiny core, while Masuda’s men surround the hall in large numbers. Action: Miyake tells frightened listeners the first union is like a prison and asks each person to bring one recruit. Payoff: The second union gains public legitimacy, police protection, and a replicable method for growth.

## 5. Cast And Terms

**Tetsuo Masuda**: Charismatic leader of Nissan’s radical first union; powerful with workers but increasingly committed to all-or-nothing confrontation.

**Katsuji Kawamata**: Industrial Bank of Japan man at Nissan; hard-line strategist who backs the second union and the lockout.

**Genshichi Asahara**: Nissan president figure portrayed as anxious, squeezed between union pressure and business criticism.

**Masaru Miyake**: Former Zero pilot and rising middle-management employee; founder of the company-friendly second union.

**Ichiro Shioji**: Young personnel-department hire, later a major Nissan labor figure; ambitious, tough, and useful in physical and political confrontation.

**Nakamura**: Masuda associate who later recognizes the union’s innocence about management power and worker limits.

**Kacho**: Section chief; middle-level shop-floor authority, first in the union, then reclassified by management as part of management.

**Bucho**: Department chief, another middle-management rank.

**Suribachi**: “Mortar and pestle”; Nissan term for kangaroo-court harassment used to grind down section chiefs.

**Nikkeiren**: Japan Federation of Employers’ Associations; coordinates employer strategy against radical unions.

**IBJ / Fuji Bank**: Financial backers whose loans help Nissan outlast the strike.

**Second union**: Company-friendly rival union organized by Miyake with management support.

## 6. What To Hold Lightly

- Kawamata’s memories of childhood austerity, toys, sweet potatoes, and cold houses matter mainly as texture for his values.
- Miyake’s detailed air-war memories are vivid, but the key point is his lesson about industrial power, numbers, and technology.
- Shioji’s radio-repair and black-market survival stories matter chiefly because they explain his toughness and opportunism.
- The comparison to American labor disputes and International Harvester is useful context, but Nissan’s immediate issue is that Japanese competitors agree not to exploit the strike.
