## Orientation Before Reading

- The chapter sits in the late 1950s, after Nissan’s labor battles and before its serious American expansion. The company has survived internal conflict but is still fragile, primitive, and unsure of itself.
- Yutaka Katayama is the hinge figure at first: aristocratic, conservative, anti-union, car-obsessed, and increasingly alienated inside Nissan because the Miyake union has become a route to power within management.
- Nissan’s internal politics matter as much as engineering. The post-Masuda settlement has not simply weakened labor; it has made the new union part of Kawamata’s control system.
- The practical question is whether a poor, technically backward Japanese automaker can find any opening in the richest and most car-saturated market in the world.
- The emotional stakes are national as well as corporate. Japan is still carrying postwar insecurity, and failure abroad feels like dishonor before it feels like experiment.
- The chapter’s title, “The First Victory,” refers not just to winning a race, but to the first psychological breach in Nissan’s sense that America is unreachable.

## The Chapter Arc

- Katayama is introduced not as a typical salaryman but as an independent, privileged, internationally exposed car enthusiast whose background leaves him less obedient to company factions.
- His anti-union stance turns from principle into career damage as the Miyake union becomes fused with Nissan’s personnel system and rewards loyal insiders.
- Katayama’s stalled career and failed Flying Feather project leave him nearly ready to quit, but the Australian rally gives him a concrete way to prove Japanese durability.
- Nissan’s fear of embarrassment gives way to a low-risk argument: Japan has little prestige in cars, so losing abroad cannot cost much, while even a surprise showing could matter.
- The rally team begins as a political imposition, with union-selected drivers, but Australia makes the drivers dependent on Katayama and turns a hostile group into a team.
- The Datsun’s crude virtues become decisive: it is not graceful, fast, or modern, but it is rugged enough for terrible roads, and that is exactly what the rally demands.
- The Australian victory makes Katayama a national hero without solving his Nissan problem; his old job is gone, his enemies remain, and celebrity makes him more visible.
- Nissan then moves from symbolic victory to commercial risk: exporting to America becomes thinkable because overseas volume could lower domestic costs and strengthen Nissan at home.
- A first engineering mission to America converts vague ambition into a terrifying operational decision: Nissan will try to sell its tough, awkward little car in the world’s strongest auto market.

## What To Watch For

- Halberstam keeps contrasting status and competence: union loyalty can win jobs, while actual car knowledge often sits with marginal or politically inconvenient people.
- “Durability” becomes Japan’s first exportable advantage. Nissan cannot yet compete on styling, speed, comfort, or sophistication, but it can build something that survives abuse.
- The chapter treats fear of failure as a major Japanese corporate constraint. Avoiding dishonor delays action even when the downside is objectively small.
- America functions as both market and classroom. Nissan is not only trying to sell there; its engineers are learning what a car must be in a high-speed, high-income society.
- Trading companies matter because Japanese manufacturers are culturally and linguistically insulated. Marubeni and Wakatsuki make foreign contact possible before Nissan itself is ready.
- The Volkswagen is the benchmark. It is foreign, postwar, successful, well-supported, and still small enough that Nissan can imagine competing with it.
- Cheap labor and workplace control are presented as temporary compensating advantages for weak capital investment and primitive machinery.
- The chapter repeatedly links export success to domestic power: selling abroad is not an end in itself, but a way to raise volume, cut costs, and win Japan’s home market.

## Load-Bearing Scenes

**Katayama against the Miyake union.**  
Setup: after the 1953 labor defeat, the new union becomes a management pipeline rather than an outside adversary. Action: Katayama refuses to align himself fully, turns down union office, and complains that the union knows board decisions before managers do. Payoff: he becomes an internal exile, clarifying how Nissan’s official hierarchy and real power structure have diverged.

**The Australian rally proposal.**  
Setup: Katayama sees a 10,000-mile rally over rough roads and recognizes a match between the terrain and the Datsun’s crude toughness. Action: he translates the rules, argues down cautious superiors, and accepts union-selected drivers he does not trust. Payoff: Nissan’s first international test begins less as confident strategy than as one man’s wager against corporate timidity.

**The rally win.**  
Setup: the Datsun lacks nearly everything associated with a good car except endurance. Action: in Australia, the team bonds under foreign pressure, and the car named Fuji wins its class. Payoff: Japan receives a badly needed postwar automotive victory, and Nissan briefly lets itself believe it might belong in the wider world.

**Wakatsuki at the Los Angeles auto show.**  
Setup: Nobe Wakatsuki has realized that in America the car is both necessity and identity. Action: he pushes Nissan to send vehicles, then confronts the ugly reality of the Datsun in person. Payoff: the American market looks bizarrely open, but the product gap is obvious; curiosity is not yet demand.

**Tanabe racing the Volkswagen.**  
Setup: Nissan’s engineers are testing a slow, heavy car on American highways and feeling the scale of American automotive power. Action: Tanabe takes offense at two Americans staring from a VW and turns a hill climb into an impromptu race. Payoff: when the Datsun pulls ahead, he glimpses a concrete competitive possibility: if they can beat Volkswagen at something, America is not impossible.

**The airport report with missing teeth.**  
Setup: the engineers return from America ready to recommend cautious export, while hiding a crash that injured Maki and shamed Hara. Action: Maki’s makeshift dental repair fails in front of Kawamata and the board. Payoff: embarrassment gives way to decision; Kawamata approves the pickup and orders fast fixes to the passenger car.

## Cast And Terms

- **Yutaka Katayama**: Nissan executive, car enthusiast, anti-union outsider, later central to Nissan’s American story.
- **Masuda union**: the militant Nissan union defeated in the earlier labor struggle.
- **Miyake union**: the replacement union, aligned with management power and Kawamata’s control.
- **Kawamata**: Nissan president, decisive but operating through a company still shaped by labor politics.
- **Datsun**: Nissan’s export car brand; in this chapter, tough, crude, underpowered, and potentially viable.
- **Flying Feather**: Katayama’s failed attempt to design a small ultralight car outside Nissan.
- **Nobe Wakatsuki**: Marubeni representative in Los Angeles who pushes Nissan toward the American market.
- **Marubeni**: major Japanese trading company acting as foreign-market intermediary.
- **Shosha**: Japanese trading company, used to bridge linguistic, cultural, and commercial distance from foreign markets.
- **Kuniyuki Tanabe**: young Nissan engineer on the first American test team, tied by marriage to Kawamata.
- **Teiichi Hara and Shin Maki**: Nissan engineers on the American test mission.
- **Gorham**: American engineer associated with early Datsun design; a symbolic link between Nissan and American automotive know-how.
- **Volkswagen**: the small-car benchmark Nissan studies, admires, and hopes to challenge.

## What To Hold Lightly

- Katayama’s colorful memories of the Brown Palace and China help sketch his personality, but they are not central to Nissan’s strategic turn.
- The speeding-ticket anecdotes in California mainly reinforce his love of cars and personal freedom.
- The ham steak misunderstanding is vivid cultural comedy; its larger use is to show the engineers’ poverty and disorientation.
- The precise ugliness of the early Datsun matters less than the broader fact that Nissan is trying to export a car built for Japanese taxi roads into American highway culture.
