Orientation Before Reading

  • Toyota’s production logic here turns on two linked disciplines: level the work before it reaches the line, then stop the work when defects appear.
  • The stakes are practical, not philosophical: inventory, late orders, supplier chaos, quality failures, and hidden problems all become expensive when flow is tight.
  • Key actors include Toyota executives Fujio Cho, Alan Cabito, Ray Tanguay, Gary Convis, and outside or adjacent figures like Shigeo Shingo, Trim Masters, GM, and suppliers learning TPS.
  • The main conflict is between conventional efficiency and Toyota efficiency. Mass production protects output numbers and batch convenience; Toyota protects system stability and quality at the source.
  • The chapter keeps returning to a hard managerial tradeoff: accepting some visible cost now, such as inventory, stoppages, or slower decisions, to prevent larger downstream waste.
  • Watch how Toyota treats tools like andon, kanban, heijunka boxes, and poka-yoke as expressions of a management system, not magic devices.

The Chapter Arc

  • The discussion begins with mixed-model production: changeovers that once blocked heijunka are redesigned away, so different engines can be built in a repeating leveled sequence.
  • Shigeo Shingo’s setup analysis changes the meaning of changeover from unavoidable downtime into a solvable problem of separating internal from external setup.
  • Leveling expands beyond the plant: suppliers like Trim Masters can run with very little inventory only because Toyota’s schedule is reliable enough to trust.
  • The chapter complicates lean orthodoxy: some finished-goods inventory can be the less wasteful choice when it protects level production from unstable customer demand.
  • Toyota then faces the build-to-order challenge and reframes it as “change to order,” preserving a leveled base schedule while allowing controlled customer-driven changes.
  • The same leveling principle moves into services and product development, where standard times, predictable milestones, staggered project loads, and trusted partners make irregular work manageable.
  • The aluminum gutter case converts the idea into a non-auto example: selective inventory, kanban, heijunka scheduling, and reduced changeover turn seasonal chaos into smoother flow.
  • The chapter then pivots to jidoka: Toyota’s next principle is that quality problems must surface immediately, even if that means stopping production.
  • The final movement shows that stopping is not mainly a technology. It depends on trained people, standard work, visible problems, and leaders who reward surfacing defects.

What To Watch For

  • **Heijunka is about volume and mix.** Toyota is not just smoothing total output; it is smoothing the sequence of product types so labor, machines, and suppliers are not whipsawed.
  • **Changeover time is a constraint that can be attacked.** The engine and stamping press examples show that leveling often becomes possible only after setup work is redesigned.
  • **Inventory is treated conditionally.** Finished goods are waste in one frame, but useful protection in another if they stabilize the whole value stream.
  • **Toyota rejects simple either/or choices.** Build-to-order is not accepted at the cost of heijunka; instead, Toyota permits bounded order changes inside a leveled system.
  • **Supplier trust is part of the production system.** Trim Masters can run lean because Toyota’s promises about mix and volume are credible most of the time.
  • **Jidoka shifts authority downward.** Team members are expected to stop or signal problems; managers are expected to ask about root cause, not blame lost output.
  • **Andon is buffered and structured.** At Toyota, a signal does not necessarily freeze the whole plant; team leaders get a short response window and line segments have buffers.
  • **Quality is built into process timing.** One-piece flow, low inventory, and short lead times make defects visible quickly, which is exactly the point.
  • **Standardization underwrites flexibility.** Whether in engineering projects or factory jobs, Toyota can flex people and detect defects because the work has defined methods.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Mixed engines on one line.**

Setup: A plant cannot level production because switching among engine sizes requires moving parts, tools, and pallets.

Action: Parts are put on flow racks, tools are mounted over the line, and a flexible pallet is created.

Payoff: Changeover disappears, making a mixed-model leveled sequence possible.

**Shingo at the stamping press.**

Setup: Huge presses traditionally take hours to change over, so batch production feels unavoidable.

Action: Shingo separates work that must happen while the press is stopped from work that can happen while it still runs.

Payoff: Hours shrink to minutes, proving that a major “fixed” constraint was partly bad work design.

**Trim Masters feeding Toyota.**

Setup: A seat supplier has only three hours from Toyota’s order broadcast to build, sequence, truck, and deliver seats.

Action: Trim Masters runs with very little inventory because it trusts Toyota’s leveled Camry and Avalon mix.

Payoff: The supplier’s lean system depends on Toyota’s schedule discipline; a spike would create emergency freight.

**Cho corrects Scafade.**

Setup: Russ Scafade believes he has succeeded because the Georgetown powertrain plant has not shut down the assembly plant for a month.

Action: Fujio Cho tells him this means problems are being hidden and urges him to remove inventory so problems surface.

Payoff: The definition of good management flips from protecting uninterrupted output to exposing and solving process weakness.

**The missing cotter pin at NUMMI.**

Setup: Liker works on an axle subassembly line where a missing cotter pin is a serious safety defect.

Action: The team traces how the error passed through multiple checks and searches for root cause and countermeasures within the two-hour window before main-line disruption.

Payoff: The scene shows Toyota’s quality culture as collective accountability, not individual blame.

**The hardware-store andon.**

Setup: A supplier orders an elaborate andon system to imitate Toyota.

Action: A Toyota mentor rejects the purchase and hands the plant manager red, yellow, and green flags.

Payoff: The point is that andon is a behavior and response system first; technology only helps after that exists.

Cast And Terms

  • **Heijunka:** Leveling production volume and product mix to smooth workload across people, machines, and suppliers.
  • **Muda, muri, mura:** Waste, overburden, and unevenness; the chapter argues lean flow requires attacking all three.
  • **Shigeo Shingo:** Industrial engineer associated with reducing setup time and distinguishing internal from external setup.
  • **Internal setup / external setup:** Work that must occur while a machine is stopped versus work that can be prepared while it is still running.
  • **Trim Masters:** Georgetown, Kentucky seat supplier delivering just-in-time to Toyota.
  • **Alan Cabito:** Toyota Motor Sales executive explaining “change to order.”
  • **Kanban:** Signal, often a card, authorizing production or replenishment based on downstream pull.
  • **Heijunka box:** Visual scheduling device that distributes kanban cards into leveled time slots.
  • **Jidoka:** Built-in quality; machines or people stop the process when abnormalities appear.
  • **Andon:** Visual or audible signal calling attention to a problem and triggering support.
  • **Poka-yoke:** Error-proofing device or method that prevents or detects mistakes.
  • **Fujio Cho:** Toyota leader used here to teach that hidden problems are worse than stoppages.
  • **NUMMI:** Toyota-GM joint venture plant in Fremont, California, used as a TPS learning site.
