Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter begins in the late 1970s, as Ford is losing not just market share and confidence but senior manufacturing talent. Marvin Runyon, a Ford assembly executive with thirty-eight years inside the company, is preparing to leave.
  • The immediate conflict is personal and institutional: Runyon is angry over Ford’s treatment of Lee Iacocca and Bill Bourke, frustrated by Ford bureaucracy, and skeptical of Philip Caldwell’s leadership.
  • Nissan is preparing to build an American plant, and its search for an American manufacturing chief turns Runyon’s retirement into a symbolic defection.
  • Smyrna, Tennessee matters because it is outside the old industrial North, in a right-to-work state, and therefore largely beyond the easy reach of the UAW.
  • The chapter uses Nissan’s Tennessee plant as a test case: can American workers, under Japanese manufacturing discipline and different labor rules, build vehicles to Japanese quality standards?
  • The second half shifts to John DeLorean. His story is not a tangent so much as a contrast: Runyon represents manufacturing seriousness; DeLorean represents celebrity, ego, financial improvisation, and the fantasy of founding a new car company in a closed industry.
  • The larger stake is Detroit’s failure to protect its own strengths. Halberstam is asking why American car culture could produce gifted people, then misplace or corrupt them.

The Chapter Arc

  • Runyon moves from loyal Ford insider to prospective outsider because Ford’s bureaucracy, labor constraints, and product decisions have worn down his belief that the company can build well.
  • Ford’s farewell to Runyon changes from ritual honor to petty suspicion once executives realize he may join Nissan, revealing how threatened Detroit feels by Japanese competition.
  • Runyon’s move to Nissan becomes less an act of betrayal than an indictment of Ford: he sees himself as joining an American factory with Japanese ownership, not abandoning American workers.
  • Nissan’s choice of Smyrna changes the labor geography of auto production by moving high-wage vehicle manufacturing away from Detroit’s unionized industrial base.
  • Runyon’s first budget dealings with Nissan change his sense of management: instead of constant signatures, second-guessing, and mid-project cuts, he finds trust and long-range commitment.
  • Nissan’s doubts about American workers shape the plant itself. The factory becomes highly automated, starts with trucks, and hires technically skilled “technicians” rather than traditional line workers.
  • Smyrna’s early success changes the argument about American labor. Under different systems, American workers produce at levels that satisfy Nissan and even impress Ishihara.
  • Runyon’s public criticism of Detroit hardens into a causal claim: American management and labor protected themselves, raised prices under tariff shelter, and neglected customers and quality.
  • The chapter then pivots to DeLorean, whose rise and collapse change the meaning of entrepreneurship in Detroit from disciplined mechanical creation to media-driven spectacle and financial scandal.

What To Watch For

  • Watch how Halberstam separates patriotism from ownership. Runyon’s new plant is Japanese-owned, but it gives Americans jobs and proves something about American manufacturing capacity.
  • Notice the contrast between Ford’s paper-heavy hierarchy and Nissan’s trust-based accountability. Ishihara’s “yes or no” approach is one of the chapter’s cleanest management claims.
  • Track the role of capital patience. Nissan can budget $500 million and accept years without profit; Runyon believes American firms would cave quickly to analyst pressure.
  • Pay attention to labor as both cause and consequence. Nissan avoids the UAW, but it pays near-UAW wages because the union has already set the market standard.
  • Smyrna is not anti-worker; it is anti-old-system. The workers are well paid, respected, and mobile, but the plant is designed to avoid traditional job classifications and union rules.
  • Automation is presented as both technological progress and distrust. Nissan automates partly because it doubts American workers and wants to limit union leverage.
  • Runyon’s critique of Detroit tariffs matters: protection from Japanese competition, in his view, should have bought price advantage and reform, but Detroit used it to preserve profits.
  • DeLorean’s story tests a different failure mode: talent without discipline, product seriousness, or financial restraint becomes another version of Detroit’s decline.
  • Watch Halberstam’s moral contrast between older car founders and DeLorean. The old founders sacrificed for the machine; DeLorean sacrifices the company to the image.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Runyon’s Ford farewell.**

Setup: a senior Ford executive is leaving after decades of service, possibly for Nissan. Action: Ford asks him to sign papers promising not to join a competitor, withholds the customary car and silver tray, then partially reverses itself. Payoff: the scene turns an executive departure into a miniature portrait of Ford’s insecurity and pettiness.

**Ishihara returns the signed budget.**

Setup: Runyon prepares a cost estimate the Ford way, expecting layers of approval. Action: Nissan president Takashi Ishihara signs it once, tells Runyon to frame it, and says future decisions will be yes or no. Payoff: Runyon discovers a management culture that prizes responsibility over procedural cover.

**The trucks-first decision.**

Setup: Nissan is nervous about whether Americans can meet Japanese quality standards. Action: Ishihara tells Runyon the plant will begin with trucks because they are Nissan’s lowest-selling item. Payoff: the bluntness exposes both Japanese doubt and the experimental nature of Smyrna.

**The Smyrna factory floor.**

Setup: Nissan builds outside the old Detroit labor system. Action: machines handle major tasks, technically skilled workers maintain them, managers work near the line, and Runyon wears the same blue uniform as everyone else. Payoff: the plant becomes Halberstam’s picture of what American auto manufacturing might have been under different rules.

**DeLorean on television.**

Setup: the former GM star has become a celebrity entrepreneur whose car company is failing. Action: Americans see FBI footage of him apparently participating in a drug deal. Payoff: even acquittal cannot restore him; the image seals his fall from industrial rebel to cautionary spectacle.

Cast And Terms

  • **Marvin Runyon**: Former Ford assembly executive who leaves to run Nissan’s American plant in Smyrna.
  • **Philip Caldwell**: Iacocca’s successor at Ford; seen by Runyon as rule-bound and lacking Ford’s deeper manufacturing instinct.
  • **Lee Iacocca**: Former Ford product leader whose firing helps alienate Runyon.
  • **Bill Bourke**: Another Ford product man whose dismissal contributes to Runyon’s disillusionment.
  • **Nissan**: Japanese automaker opening an American factory to build vehicles inside the U.S. market.
  • **Takashi Ishihara**: President of Nissan; blunt, manufacturing-centered, and willing to trust Runyon with real authority.
  • **Smyrna, Tennessee**: Site of Nissan’s U.S. plant, chosen for logistics, incentives, right-to-work laws, and distance from UAW power.
  • **UAW**: United Auto Workers, the dominant auto union in Detroit’s industrial system.
  • **Glass House**: Ford headquarters; shorthand here for centralized corporate control and budget cutting.
  • **John DeLorean**: Former GM star and founder of the DeLorean Motor Company, whose collapse becomes a symbol of celebrity-era industrial failure.
  • **DeLorean Motor Company**: DeLorean’s attempted sports-car startup, built with heavy British support in Northern Ireland.
  • **Oligopoly**: A market dominated by a few large firms; here, Detroit’s Big Three auto structure.

What To Hold Lightly

  • The state-by-state courtship of Nissan, including governors’ gestures, is colorful but mainly supports the point that jobs were scarce and valuable.
  • DeLorean’s clothes, marriages, surgery, and celebrity friendships matter as texture, but the central issue is the distance between image and product.
  • The exact salary comparisons at Smyrna are less important than the structural fact: Nissan paid well without accepting the old union framework.
  • The missing silver tray is memorable, but its function is symbolic. The real issue is Ford’s reaction to a senior manufacturing man choosing the competitor.
