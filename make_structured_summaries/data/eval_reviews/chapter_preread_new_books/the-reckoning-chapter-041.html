Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter centers on 1979-1981, when Japanese auto exports to the United States become a political crisis rather than just a trade success.
  • Naohiro Amaya of MITI is the key figure: a Japanese bureaucrat unusually trusted by Americans because he understands their politics and does not posture about Japan’s weakness.
  • The American auto industry is in visible distress: unemployment is high, interest rates are punishing, gas prices have changed consumer demand, and Detroit’s large-car strategy looks badly exposed.
  • Washington is divided. Free-trade economists oppose import limits, but politicians facing unemployed auto workers and anti-Japanese pressure want relief.
  • Japanese automakers, especially Toyota and Nissan, believe they earned their success without owing MITI much. They see export limits as punishment for American failure.
  • The conflict is not simply America versus Japan. It is also MITI versus Japanese corporate pride, free-trade principle versus political survival, and national strategy versus company ambition.

The Chapter Arc

  • Amaya’s reputation shifts from awkward technocrat to indispensable negotiator; he becomes the person both sides believe can read the other without illusion.
  • The American auto problem changes from a sectoral complaint into a national political issue, making voluntary Japanese restraint more likely.
  • Carter’s administration signals that Japan should limit exports, but the Japanese delay because the U.S. election and transition may change the bargaining field.
  • Reagan enters with free-market instincts, yet his administration discovers that ideology cannot fully absorb the pressure from Detroit’s collapse.
  • Japanese Diet members visit Washington expecting familiar American softness and return shocked by the new protectionist mood.
  • Serious bargaining begins once Japan realizes that voluntary restraint may be the only way to avoid harsher American legislation.
  • Amaya frames the deal around two concrete protections for Japan: a fixed numerical ceiling and freedom to export cars by category, not just small cheap models.
  • The hardest negotiation moves from Washington to Tokyo, where Amaya must force angry Japanese auto executives to accept limits on their own triumph.
  • The final ceiling of 1.685 million cars marks a symbolic reversal: Japan, once defeated by American industrial power, now restrains its exports to preserve the political and economic balance with America.

What To Watch For

  • Amaya’s strength comes from candor. Halberstam presents trust as a negotiating asset, especially in a system where both sides expect gamesmanship.
  • The American request is called “voluntary,” but the pressure behind it is legislative threat. Japan chooses restraint because the alternative is likely worse.
  • The American free-trade position is internally unstable: economists oppose protection, but elected officials and trade negotiators must manage unemployment, anger, and Congress.
  • Detroit’s troubles are not treated as purely Japanese-caused. Halberstam emphasizes American management choices, labor costs, oil shocks, consumer alienation, and interest rates.
  • MITI’s authority is changing. Earlier Japanese industries had depended heavily on state guidance, but the automakers now claim their success as their own.
  • The fixed-number ceiling matters because it protects Japan if the U.S. market shrinks further. A percentage cap would make Japanese exports fall with American sales.
  • The category issue matters because Japanese companies want to move upward into more profitable middle-range cars, not remain trapped in small-car exports.
  • Amaya’s appeal to national memory is deliberate. By invoking Yosuke Matsuoka and the League of Nations, he casts corporate defiance as a path toward dangerous nationalism.
  • The chapter’s deepest claim is that Japan’s success depends on American strength. Amaya restrains Japan not out of deference, but to preserve the system that allowed Japan to rise.

Load-Bearing Scenes

  • **Amaya across the table:** Early in his career, Americans see him as awkward, reading stiff English in a poor suit. Over time he becomes elegant, fluent, and witty. The payoff is that his personal evolution mirrors Japan’s postwar confidence.
  • **Washington receives the Diet delegation:** Japanese politicians arrive expecting to resist pressure as they had before. They encounter anger from old critics, lost protection from former allies, and a broader mood of anti-Japanese resentment. The payoff is Japan’s realization that delay is dangerous.
  • **Brock’s cool warning:** Bill Brock tells the Japanese he supports free trade but must face political reality. He presents himself as their friend, warning that people like Senator Danforth are gaining power. The payoff is a soft-spoken threat: cooperate now or face something harsher.
  • **Amaya calculates the ceiling:** He estimates what Toyota and Nissan need to remain profitable and what America needs to regain pride and time. The payoff is the deal’s architecture: high enough for Japan, visible enough for America.
  • **The Ginza meeting with Ishihara:** Amaya meets Nissan’s Ishihara, hears his anger, then bows on the tatami and begs him not to repeat Japan’s nationalist mistakes. The payoff is Ishihara’s concession, because company pride cannot override national obligation.
  • **The historical reversal:** Amaya reflects that in 1945 he could not have imagined limiting Japanese auto exports to Henry Ford’s country. The payoff is the chapter’s title: an era of American industrial dominance has ended, but Amaya wants America strong enough to sustain the postwar order.

Cast And Terms

  • **Naohiro Amaya:** Senior MITI official and central negotiator; trusted by Americans and powerful enough to pressure Japanese automakers.
  • **MITI:** Japan’s Ministry of International Trade and Industry, the state institution associated with postwar industrial strategy.
  • **Toyota and Nissan:** Japan’s two leading automakers in this chapter; both resist export restraints, with Toyota especially aggressive in public attacks.
  • **Ishihara:** Head of Nissan and the Automobile Manufacturers Association; personally angered by limits but ultimately forced to concede.
  • **Automobile Manufacturers Association:** Japanese industry body led by Ishihara, representing automaker interests.
  • **Jimmy Carter:** U.S. president during the first phase of pressure; reluctant to aid Detroit but politically aware of auto unemployment.
  • **Ronald Reagan:** Incoming president in 1981; ideologically free-market but unable to ignore the auto crisis.
  • **Charles Schultze and Murray Weidenbaum:** Carter and Reagan economic advisers, respectively; both oppose import restrictions as bad economics.
  • **Bill Brock:** Reagan administration trade negotiator; pushes the Japanese toward voluntary limits while presenting himself as their ally.
  • **Alexander Haig:** Secretary of State, involved in jurisdictional conflict over who should handle negotiations.
  • **UAW:** United Auto Workers, the American auto labor union seeking relief for the industry.
  • **John Danforth:** Missouri senator and prominent critic of Japanese auto imports.
  • **Voluntary export restraint:** A negotiated Japanese limit on exports to avoid formal U.S. protectionist legislation.
  • **Yosuke Matsuoka:** Japanese diplomat who led Japan out of the League of Nations in the 1930s; invoked as a warning against nationalist defiance.

What To Hold Lightly

  • Amaya’s clothes and English improve over time; the details are vivid mainly because they dramatize Japan’s growing confidence.
  • The jokes about economics Ph.D.s and Nobel laureates show Amaya’s wit, but they are not central to the trade bargain.
  • The insults aimed at Amaya in the Japanese press reveal corporate anger, though the exact phrasing matters less than the intensity.
  • The comparison to the surrender aboard the battleship Missouri is symbolic; the practical mechanism is still the export ceiling and the threat of American legislation.
