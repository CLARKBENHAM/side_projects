Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter sits in the early 1970s, when Detroit’s auto executives and union leaders are beginning to see that the postwar boom is ending.
  • The pressure is coming from several directions at once: Japanese competition, rising American labor costs, heavier worker expectations, and a younger workforce less loyal to both company and union.
  • Doug Fraser, a top UAW leader and future president, is the chapter’s central figure. Halberstam uses him as a bridge between the Depression-era union world and the alienated workers of the seventies.
  • The UAW is no longer just fighting management. It is also trying to understand why its own members, especially younger ones, treat union gains as ordinary entitlements rather than hard-won protections.
  • The conflict is partly generational: older workers remember insecurity, arbitrary firings, and degrading shop-floor authority; younger workers compare factory life to television, college students, executives, and cleaner white-collar jobs.
  • The deeper stake is whether American autoworkers, used to abundance and bargaining power, can adapt before more disciplined foreign competitors threaten the whole system.

The Chapter Arc

  • The chapter opens with a shift in mood: a few executives and union leaders realize the industry is entering a harsher era, even though most of Detroit still resists pessimism.
  • Fraser emerges as the person most alert to a new kind of worker alienation, one aimed not only at companies but also at the UAW leadership itself.
  • Halberstam then rewinds to Fraser’s childhood and early work life, showing why he values union protections so intensely: Depression hunger, evictions, arbitrary firing, and shop-floor humiliation formed his politics.
  • Fraser’s early factory experience turns the union from an idea into a practical defense of dignity, especially against petty authoritarian control.
  • His confrontation with foreman Ed Remsnyder becomes the formative victory: the workers learn that disciplined collective action can limit managerial power.
  • The chapter then pivots back to the late sixties and early seventies, when the old “us against them” union politics no longer works as cleanly.
  • Younger workers begin treating the union as another distant institution, blaming it for frustrations it cannot solve and discounting the benefits it can deliver.
  • Incidents at Chrysler make the change concrete: angry local meetings, rising absenteeism, indifference to double-time work, and workers who simply refuse the old bargain of more hours for more pay.
  • The chapter ends by widening the frame: Fraser sees the American factory as a miniature version of the country, and he contrasts its loosened discipline with the ordered intensity he observes in Japan.

What To Watch For

  • Watch how Halberstam connects affluence to fragility. The prosperity that strengthened the UAW also weakens memory, gratitude, and institutional loyalty.
  • Notice that Fraser’s complaint is not just moral. He sees alienation as a strategic danger because American workers are facing competitors who are more disciplined and purposeful.
  • Pay attention to the difference between wages and dignity. Fraser repeatedly treats dignity, not pay, as the union’s deepest historical achievement.
  • Track how expectations become fixed. After decades of growth, both managers and workers assume rising markets, rising pay, and rising benefits are normal.
  • Watch the shift from scarcity politics to comparison politics. Younger workers do not compare themselves to Depression workers; they compare themselves to executives, students, TV families, and white-collar employees.
  • The UAW’s authority is under pressure from success. Once benefits are normalized, the union looks less like a liberator and more like part of the system.
  • Absenteeism matters here as evidence, not just complaint. It shows a changed relationship to work, money, family obligation, and factory discipline.
  • The Japanese plant scene is meant to sting: Halberstam lets Fraser admire American independence while also fearing its economic consequences.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Fraser’s Depression childhood**

Setup: Fraser grows up in Detroit in a union household, watching his father lose jobs and the family struggle for food and rent.

Action: Halberstam lingers on stale bread fried with onions, later remembered nostalgically but tasting awful when recreated.

Payoff: The scene grounds Fraser’s politics in lived scarcity rather than ideology alone.

**The glass toilet at Everhot**

Setup: Fraser works for low wages in a workplace where supervisors monitor workers with degrading precision.

Action: The toilet is glass above the waist so a supervisor can see how long a man has been inside.

Payoff: Fraser’s memory of humiliation explains why he sees the union’s central gift as dignity.

**The Remsnyder slowdown**

Setup: A feared general foreman orders Fraser’s shift to raise output from fourteen fenders a day to sixteen, despite bad steel and bad machines.

Action: Fraser organizes a disciplined slowdown to twelve fenders, with everyone working visibly hard and giving management no excuse to fire anyone.

Payoff: Remsnyder retreats to fourteen. Fraser learns he can lead, and the workers learn authority can be checked.

**Cliff Earl’s transfer**

Setup: Earl’s job is to hear grievances from Chrysler locals, once a prized assignment.

Action: Meetings become so hostile that Earl asks Fraser for another job and is moved to work with retirees.

Payoff: One job change becomes a generational crossing: older retirees still treat the union as an ally; younger workers treat it as an enemy.

**The Sterling Heights turnout**

Setup: After a Chrysler strike, the company urgently needs production and offers double-time Saturday and Sunday work.

Action: Only half the workers show up, despite the chance to earn serious extra money.

Payoff: Both company and union leaders realize the old assumption that workers always want more hours for more pay no longer holds.

**The Tokyo plant walk-through**

Setup: Fraser tours a Japanese plant with a labor leader and a management official near lunch break.

Action: At a manager’s raised hand, workers stop and wait until the visitors pass.

Payoff: Fraser contrasts Japanese discipline with American shop-floor independence, feeling both pride and unease.

Cast And Terms

  • **Doug Fraser**: UAW vice-president, later president; central witness to the generational shift among autoworkers.
  • **UAW**: United Automobile Workers, the union that won major gains for American autoworkers during the postwar boom.
  • **Walter Reuther**: Legendary UAW leader; Fraser served as a top aide and was long seen as his eventual successor.
  • **Leonard Woodcock**: UAW president after Reuther; expected to retire in 1974, clearing the way for Fraser.
  • **Chrysler division**: Fraser’s area of union responsibility during several key incidents in the chapter.
  • **Cliff Earl**: UAW official who handles member grievances and becomes a barometer of younger workers’ hostility.
  • **Bill O’Brien**: Chrysler labor vice-president who alerts Fraser to the poor double-time turnout at Sterling Heights.
  • **Ed Remsnyder**: DeSoto plant general foreman whose authority Fraser challenges in his first major union victory.
  • **Dodge Main, DeSoto, Sterling Heights, Twinsburg, Jefferson Avenue**: Chrysler-linked plants used to show different forms of factory life, labor pressure, and worker alienation.
  • **Absenteeism**: A key symptom of changed worker attitudes, especially among younger workers less bound by older habits of necessity.
