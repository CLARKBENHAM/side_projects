1. Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter sits in 1985-86, after Ford’s near-death early-eighties crisis and after Japan’s automakers have become the central test of American industrial strength.
  • Ed Lundy has left Ford’s board, but his finance-first culture remains embedded through protégés such as Red Poling and Allan Gilmour.
  • Ford is again split between product people and finance people. The question is whether Ford should keep building small cars itself or hand much of that work to Mazda.
  • Don Petersen represents the argument that Ford must remain a car-making company, not merely a marketer of cars sourced elsewhere.
  • Lee Iacocca, now at Chrysler, raids Ford for Bob Lutz, turning an internal Ford succession problem into a Chrysler opportunity.
  • In Japan, Nissan’s old power structure is passing: Katsuji Kawamata dies, while Ichiro Shioji, once central to Nissan’s labor-management system, is excluded and diminished.
  • The larger frame is macroeconomic: the yen rises sharply, oil prices fall, Japanese exporters face pressure, and Halberstam uses the moment to widen the argument about America’s industrial future.

2. The Chapter Arc

  • Lundy exits formal authority at Ford, but his real legacy shifts from officeholding to institutional culture: finance discipline still governs through the men he promoted.
  • Ford’s internal fight moves from personality-driven conflict to strategic identity: the issue is no longer who wins inside the company, but whether Ford should surrender small-car production.
  • Red Poling’s cost logic turns Mazda from partner into possible substitute for Ford’s own manufacturing base, raising the stakes from savings to plant closures and industrial capacity.
  • Petersen’s resistance reframes small cars as a battlefield Ford cannot abandon without becoming dependent on Japan and losing its own manufacturing purpose.
  • Iacocca hires Bob Lutz away from Ford, changing Lutz from a blocked internal rival into a Chrysler asset and exposing Ford’s fear of charismatic product men.
  • At Nissan, Kawamata’s death converts an old regime into memory, while Shioji’s absence from the public funeral shows that power can vanish even before a person disappears.
  • The strengthened yen changes Japan’s export advantage: Japanese automakers must raise prices, squeeze costs, and rely more heavily on quality, styling, and system discipline.
  • Halberstam then turns the currency shock into a broader comparison: Japan adapts under pressure, while America debates politics, debt, services, education, and expectations without facing the industrial problem directly.
  • The chapter ends by shifting from corporate history to national reckoning: America still has huge advantages, but may lack the discipline to use them equitably in a leaner global economy.

3. What To Watch For

  • The persistence of Lundy’s influence matters more than his retirement. Halberstam is showing how corporate cultures outlive the people who create them.
  • The Mazda question is not just outsourcing. It is about whether Ford can remain a manufacturer in the segments where Japanese competition is strongest.
  • Petersen’s argument depends on uncertainty: Mazda’s future quality, exchange rates, and Japan’s competitive position may not remain fixed.
  • Lutz’s move to Chrysler repeats an old Detroit pattern: Ford fears the internal celebrity of a product man, while Iacocca knows how to use one.
  • Shioji’s exclusion from Kawamata’s public funeral is a quiet institutional purge. Watch how reputation, press pressure, and access replace direct confrontation.
  • The yen’s rise reverses an earlier Japanese advantage, but Halberstam does not treat it as a permanent rescue for American automakers.
  • The Japanese response to pain is systemic: pay cuts, speedups, supplier pressure, bank flexibility, and manufacturing improvement all push toward renewed competitiveness.
  • Halberstam contrasts political America with economic Japan. The claim is not that America lacks resources, but that it lacks shared discipline and clear priorities.
  • The “service economy” discussion is really about class structure: replacing blue-collar industrial jobs with low-wage service work threatens the broad middle class.

4. Load-Bearing Scenes

**Lundy after Ford.**

Setup: Ed Lundy has officially retired from Ford’s board after decades of influence. Action: Halberstam shows him living a carefully ordered private life, giving money meticulously, traveling with old Ford friends, and remaining surrounded by finance protégés. Payoff: the scene establishes that Ford’s finance culture no longer needs Lundy in the building to keep shaping decisions.

**The Ford-Mazda fight.**

Setup: Ford is profitable again but still struggling with the economics of small cars. Action: Poling pushes toward turning compact and subcompact production over to Mazda, while Petersen argues that Ford must not abandon the field. Payoff: the conflict becomes a test of whether American automakers will preserve manufacturing competence or retreat into branding and distribution.

**Iacocca takes Lutz.**

Setup: Bob Lutz has become too visible inside Ford, admired by journalists and subordinates in a way that recalls young Iacocca. Action: Ford slows him down; Iacocca hires him for Chrysler. Payoff: Ford loses a product star because it cannot comfortably contain him, while Chrysler gains talent and creates new tension with Hal Sperlich.

**Kawamata’s funeral without Shioji.**

Setup: Kawamata dies as one of the founding figures of Japan’s modern auto industry. Action: Nissan’s current leadership signals that Shioji should not attend the public funeral, warning that hostile photographers will pursue him. Payoff: the old Nissan labor-political order is shown as finished; even the man who helped make Kawamata powerful is pushed to the margins.

**The yen shock.**

Setup: Western trading partners want a stronger yen to correct trade imbalance, and oil prices unexpectedly fall. Action: the yen strengthens sharply, hurting Japanese exporters and forcing automakers to raise prices and cut costs. Payoff: Halberstam presents a temporary Japanese setback that may ultimately make the Japanese system even tougher.
