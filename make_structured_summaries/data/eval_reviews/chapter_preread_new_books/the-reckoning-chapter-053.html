Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter is set in 1985, after Nissan/Datsun’s American triumph is already visible: millions of cars sold, Southern California saturated with Nissans, and the old improvised sales operation replaced by polished national advertising.
  • Halberstam shifts from institutional combat to three aging Japanese figures: Yutaka Katayama, Sanosuke Tanaka, and Naohiro Amaya. Each carries a different memory of Japan’s rise.
  • The stakes are no longer only auto sales. The chapter asks whether Japan’s postwar system can adapt to affluence, global scrutiny, and a more complex high-technology economy.
  • Katayama’s return to Los Angeles is a private reunion with public evidence of success, but Nissan headquarters’ absence signals that the old visionary remains marginal to the official company memory.
  • Tanaka’s visit to his home village turns Japan’s boom into a social question: what happens when rural discipline, scarcity, and modesty give way to suburbia, money, and youth display?
  • Amaya’s section widens the frame to politics, trade, education, and national adaptability. He sees Japan’s manufacturing strength as real but worries that its political system is too rigid to manage success.
  • The central conflict is between the old virtues that built postwar Japan and the new flexibility needed to survive the world Japan has helped create.

The Chapter Arc

  • Katayama returns to Los Angeles for a twenty-fifth-anniversary celebration, and his early, precarious Datsun experiment is remeasured as a huge American commercial success.
  • The reunion changes the scale of memory: shoebox accounting, cheap Hawaii rentals, and handmade commercials now sit beside eight million Nissan sales and a mature American dealer culture.
  • Katayama’s personal stature is affirmed by old friends, but Nissan headquarters’ absence changes the celebration from corporate honor into something more intimate and slightly orphaned.
  • The chapter moves from Katayama’s restless cosmopolitanism to Tanaka’s partial retirement, where old factory discipline survives as motivational teaching for young Nissan workers.
  • Tanaka’s return to Takabeya changes the meaning of postwar land reform and rural poverty: the old landowners have prospered again, not through farming, but through real estate as cities expand.
  • Takabeya itself changes from village to suburb, and Tanaka’s discomfort turns economic success into cultural unease, especially when he sees young people dressed in imported or theatrical styles.
  • Tanaka’s final judgment softens but does not disappear: he worries about youth and affluence, yet still believes Japanese duty will reassert itself when needed.
  • Amaya’s section changes the chapter from memory to diagnosis. Rising U.S.-Japan tensions become evidence of Japan’s political inability to open itself and adapt.
  • The chapter ends with Amaya treating American pressure as a possible historical force, comparable to Perry’s arrival and the Occupation: painful outside pressure may be what forces Japan to modernize again.

What To Watch For

  • Watch how Halberstam uses old men as living evidence. Katayama, Tanaka, and Amaya are not nostalgic ornaments; they are measuring instruments for what Japan gained and what it may be losing.
  • Katayama’s absence from Nissan’s official honor roll matters. The company benefited from his imagination in America but still reflects the hierarchy and politics that once pushed him aside.
  • Tanaka’s village drive turns modernization into landscape: farms become suburbs, landowners become real estate beneficiaries, and old class resentments survive under new prosperity.
  • The youth clothing scenes are doing more than comic work. They show American influence returning to Japan not as occupation policy or industrial method, but as consumer style and self-display.
  • Amaya’s education critique is central. Rote learning produced disciplined factory workers, but he thinks high technology and global business require independence, analysis, and creativity.
  • The trade conflict is framed partly as Japan’s own failure. Amaya blames Japanese protectionism and political rigidity, not just American resentment or misunderstanding.
  • Agriculture is the key domestic obstacle in Amaya’s argument. Vulnerable farmers and their political patrons can block reforms whose costs are small nationally but symbolically powerful internationally.
  • “Hardware” and “software” are Amaya’s terms. Hardware means productive capacity: engineers, workers, skills, resources. Software means political and social adaptability.
  • The chapter’s historical comparison matters: 1853, the postwar Occupation, and 1985 trade pressure are linked as moments when foreign pressure may force Japan to do what its own system resists.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Katayama in Los Angeles:**

Setup: He returns at seventy-six, out of breath but still moving, for a party marking twenty-five years since his arrival in America. Action: He sees billboards, commercials, and the enormous Datsun/Nissan presence that grew from improvised beginnings. Payoff: The scene confirms his achievement while making Nissan headquarters’ absence feel pointed.

**Breakfast with Mayfield Marshall:**

Setup: Katayama and Marshall remember the early American operation before the brand had scale or prestige. Action: They recall shoebox bills, Hawaii promotions, and Katayama urging Marshall to buy an early Seiko watch partly to help a young Japanese salesman. Payoff: The memory links Japanese export hustle, personal networks, and faith in future mass markets.

**Tanaka at Takabeya:**

Setup: Tanaka visits his brothers and parents’ graves, then looks at the old landowner’s house. Action: He explains how former landowners stayed rich by selling land as the area urbanized. Payoff: Postwar change does not erase hierarchy; it transforms old wealth into new real estate advantage.

**Tanaka sees the teenagers:**

Setup: He drives through what used to be farmland in a large, electronic-laden Nissan Laurel. Action: He reacts sharply to a punk-styled boy and a girl dressed like an American bobby-soxer. Payoff: The car embodies industrial success, while the young people embody the cultural consequences he cannot fully accept.

**Amaya on Japan’s software:**

Setup: Trade tensions are rising, and Amaya is pushing education reform while criticizing entrenched political interests. Action: He contrasts Japan’s manufacturing “hardware” with its weak adaptive “software.” Payoff: The chapter’s deepest claim emerges: Japan’s strength in production may be undermined by its inability to tell itself hard truths and change politically.

Cast And Terms

  • **Yutaka Katayama:** Former head of Nissan’s American operation and central figure in Datsun’s U.S. success; elderly here, celebrated by old colleagues but not by Nissan headquarters.
  • **Mayfield Marshall:** Katayama’s old friend and early colleague in the American Datsun effort.
  • **John Parker:** Early Datsun advertising figure whose crude family-shot commercials contrast with Nissan’s polished 1985 campaigns.
  • **Sanosuke Tanaka:** Veteran Nissan worker and labor figure, now partly retired, still teaching loyalty and work discipline in factories.
  • **Takabeya:** Tanaka’s home village, now absorbed into the suburban spread around Isehara and Yokohama.
  • **Isehara / Yokohama:** Nearby urban centers whose expansion shows how rural Japan has been swallowed by suburban development.
  • **Naohiro Amaya:** Reform-minded Japanese official and critic of Japan’s rigid political, educational, and trade systems.
  • **Nakasone:** Japan’s prime minister, portrayed as aware of the trade dilemma but constrained by his own party.
  • **Reagan:** U.S. president; his meetings with Nakasone frame the American pressure over Japanese protectionism.
  • **Hardware / software:** Amaya’s distinction between productive capacity and political-social adaptability.
  • **Agricultural protectionism:** Japan’s protection of farmers and farm markets, treated here as a major source of foreign anger and domestic obstruction.
  • **Matthew Perry / black ships:** Reference to the 1853 American arrival that forced Japan to confront Western power and modernize.

  • The Seiko watch anecdote is vivid because it captures early export confidence; the exact watch detail matters less than Katayama’s instinct for future Japanese consumer success.
  • Tanaka’s Nissan Laurel is symbolic, but the model and gadgetry are less important than the contrast between old austerity and new affluence.
  • The teenagers’ clothes are memorable, but treat them as signs of cultural anxiety rather than as a full diagnosis of Japanese youth.
  • The Paris-to-Peking rally dream shows Katayama’s continuing romantic energy, but it sits at the edge of the chapter’s main concern: how older Japanese builders read the world they helped make.
