Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter moves from Toyota’s founders to Ford, treating production systems as inherited ideas that must be reworked for local conditions.
  • Taiichi Ohno presents Toyoda Sakichi and Toyoda Kiichiro as practical strategists: inventors, builders, and business thinkers rather than abstract theorists.
  • The central conflict is Japan’s need to build a domestic automobile industry without simply copying American mass production.
  • Toyota’s early constraints are severe: weak materials supply, limited capital, small domestic demand, handcraft habits, and pressure from foreign automakers.
  • Ford is not treated as the enemy. Ohno argues that later mass production may have misunderstood Ford’s original intent.
  • The chapter keeps returning to one question: how can production serve real demand without being trapped by inventory, speed, scale, or tradition?

The Chapter Arc

  • Toyota’s automobile project is framed as a continuation of Toyoda Sakichi’s loom work, not a separate venture.
  • Kiichiro’s “Toyotaism” turns automobile manufacturing from a hobby-like experiment into a national industrial obligation.
  • The materials problem becomes foundational: Toyota cannot build reliable engines without controlling or deeply understanding steel, casting, and sheet metal.
  • Capital discipline emerges as a production principle: simple factories are acceptable, but the right machines and tools are nonnegotiable.
  • Kiichiro’s search for a Japanese production technique shifts the problem from “copy America” to “combine presses, hand-finishing, and local capability.”
  • Government protection helps domestic carmakers, but Kiichiro warns that patriotism and law cannot replace competitive price and quality.
  • Ohno broadens the lineage from Sakichi to Kiichiro to Toyota’s present system, arguing that just-in-time grows out of Japanese originality under constraint.
  • The focus then turns to Ford: Ohno separates Ford’s true work-flow thinking from the later American habit of large lots and inventory.
  • The chapter ends by positioning Toyota’s small lots, quick setups, prevention, and demand-responsive flow as a system suited to low-growth conditions.

What To Watch For

  • Watch how often “Japanese originality” means practical adaptation under scarcity, not cultural pride alone.
  • Materials are treated as strategy. Engine quality, cost, research, and independence all depend on having the right materials at the right time.
  • Kiichiro’s equipment logic matters: save on buildings and unnecessary spending, but do not compromise on machines that determine quality.
  • The critique of inventory is not just financial. Inventory hides breakdowns, poor setup, bad scheduling, and weak prevention.
  • Ohno’s reading of Ford is deliberately revisionist: Ford’s flow system becomes a precedent for Toyota, while later Fordism becomes the cautionary example.
  • Small lot size and quick setup are linked. Toyota cannot level production or follow demand unless changeovers become fast enough to stop dictating batch size.
  • Standards must come from production knowledge. Ohno uses Ford to reject standards imposed from outside or above.
  • “Prevention is better than healing” connects maintenance to just-in-time: without buffers, machine reliability becomes a core operating requirement.
  • Low growth changes what counts as efficient. Quantity and speed look efficient when everything sells, but become waste when demand slows.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Kiichiro receives Sakichi’s automobile charge.**

Setup: Sakichi has built his life around looms and Japanese invention. Action: he tells Kiichiro to serve the country with automobiles, and later directs loom patent money toward automobile research. Payoff: Toyota’s car business appears as an inherited industrial mission, not a speculative side project.

**The materials question leads to Sendai.**

Setup: Kiichiro knows engines cannot improve if materials remain unreliable. Action: he asks Professor Honda Kotaro whether Japan can make the required materials without foreign help. Payoff: the answer gives him confidence to build a steel mill and treat materials capability as part of automobile manufacturing itself.

**Barracks factories, expensive machines.**

Setup: Toyota has limited funds and impatient investors, but automobile production demands accuracy. Action: Kiichiro cuts small wastes, tolerates simple facilities, and spends heavily on essential equipment after examining machines abroad. Payoff: the chapter establishes a recurring Toyota distinction between visible frugality and strategic investment.

**The first Ford moving-line experiment.**

Setup: Ford’s assembly problem is handling materials around a stationary chassis. Action: Sorensen and Lewis arrange parts along a path and pull the chassis forward as work is added. Payoff: Ohno accepts Ford’s flow insight as fundamental, then argues Toyota’s leap is to extend flow without warehouses.

**The large press setup fight.**

Setup: Conventional production treats die changes as costly interruptions, so large lots seem natural. Action: Toyota pushes workers to reduce setup times from hours to under an hour, then toward minutes. Payoff: quick setup makes small lots practical and turns a production nuisance into a system requirement.

**The machine stops under kanban.**

Setup: Just-in-time removes the safety stock that would normally absorb equipment trouble. Action: Ohno asks what happens when a later process comes to pick up parts and the earlier machine is down. Payoff: the answer is preventive maintenance: Toyota must prevent trouble rather than store inventory against it.

Cast And Terms

  • **Taiichi Ohno**: Toyota executive and author, explaining the logic and origins of the Toyota Production System.
  • **Toyoda Sakichi**: Founder-inventor associated with automatic looms; presented as the source of Toyota’s inventive and national-industrial spirit.
  • **Toyoda Kiichiro**: Sakichi’s son and founder of Toyota’s automobile effort; linked by Ohno to just-in-time thinking.
  • **Toyotaism**: Kiichiro’s practical doctrine for building cars for the public, at reasonable prices, with sales, materials, and industry foundations in view.
  • **Honda Kotaro**: Professor consulted on whether Japan could produce the needed automotive materials.
  • **Mishima Tokushichi**: Researcher asked to study sheet metal; associated with Japanese materials invention.
  • **Ford system**: Flow-based mass production pioneered by Henry Ford and collaborators.
  • **Charles E. Sorensen**: Ford production leader whose account of the moving assembly line Ohno quotes.
  • **Alfred P. Sloan Jr.**: General Motors leader associated with the full-line policy and market diversification.
  • **Just-in-time**: Producing and supplying only what is needed, when needed, in the needed amount.
  • **Kanban**: The operating signal system by which later processes withdraw parts from earlier processes.
  • **Autonomation**: Toyota’s machine-with-human-judgment principle, where machines stop or expose abnormalities instead of blindly producing defects.
  • **Production leveling**: Smoothing product mix and volume so earlier processes are not forced into wasteful fluctuation.
  • **Quick setup**: Rapid die or tooling changeover, necessary for small lots and mixed production.

What To Hold Lightly

  • The exact yen and dollar figures matter less than the pattern: scarce capital was spent selectively.
  • The patriotic language is vivid, but the practical issue is whether domestic cars can compete on price and quality.
  • The chessplayer metaphor is memorable but secondary to the concrete claim that Sakichi and Kiichiro saw whole systems.
  • Ford’s comments on food, health, and hospitals are mainly an analogy for prevention in production.
  • The detailed list of 1920s market changes matters chiefly because it explains why GM’s variety strategy complicated production.
