Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter sits after Chrysler’s rescue has become a public success and before the political speculation around Lee Iacocca fully hardens into a national phenomenon.
  • The central conflict is not Chrysler versus Ford, but Iacocca versus Henry Ford II in Iacocca’s imagination. Ford mostly refuses to fight back, which makes the feud asymmetrical and more revealing.
  • Iacocca is becoming a civic, commercial, and media celebrity: Chrysler commercials, the turnaround story, his memoir, magazine covers, public speeches, and the Statue of Liberty campaign all feed the same image.
  • Halberstam is testing the “new American hero” label. Iacocca’s real achievement is not denied, but the chapter keeps asking what kind of culture turns a corporate executive into a national savior.
  • Henry Ford II is no longer running Ford day to day, but he still controls key symbolic and personnel matters. He is retired, physically absent, yet institutionally present.
  • Detroit’s private codes matter: loyalty, silence after dismissal, deference to family ownership, and the rule that a well-paid executive does not publicly savage the house that paid him.
  • The chapter links business, celebrity, politics, and psychology. Iacocca’s public ascent is inseparable from his private wound over being fired.

The Chapter Arc

  • Iacocca’s Chrysler success is reframed as vengeance against Henry Ford II, not merely corporate rescue; the emotional engine behind his public triumph becomes visible.
  • The old Ford firing becomes an unresolved grievance rather than a closed career episode, with Iacocca repeatedly returning to the idea that he was sacrificed to protect Edsel Ford II.
  • Detroit social life starts reorganizing around the feud: invitations, birthday parties, bonuses, and gossip become ways of measuring proximity to Ford or Iacocca.
  • Henry Ford’s refusal to answer Iacocca changes the conflict from a duel into a display of status. Ford can ignore the attacks because his name and position insulate him.
  • Ford’s retirement reveals a different kind of power: he leaves daily management, but still shapes board appointments, leadership succession, family control, archives, property, and social boundaries.
  • Iacocca’s memoir turns from a personal score-settling vehicle into a mass-market event, selling far beyond anyone’s expectations and converting Detroit grievance into national entertainment.
  • The book’s success changes Iacocca’s category: he is no longer only a businessman but a telecelebrity, a best-selling author, a speaker, and a possible political figure.
  • The admiration around him begins to contain unease. Friends notice that the traits he criticized in Henry Ford are appearing in Iacocca’s own behavior.
  • By the end, presidential speculation becomes both plausible and comic: Iacocca denies interest, but the denial itself is treated as part of the performance.

What To Watch For

  • Watch how Halberstam separates achievement from self-interpretation. Iacocca really did help save Chrysler, but the chapter resists letting him define the meaning of that success alone.
  • Notice the importance of silence. Henry Ford’s decision not to respond is not passivity; it is a form of power, rooted in family name, wealth, and social position.
  • Track the Detroit norm of “stuffing his mouth with dollar bills”: large settlements are supposed to buy discretion. Iacocca’s memoir violates that implicit bargain.
  • Watch how celebrity turns grievance into scale. What might have remained an auto-industry feud becomes a national story because television, publishing, and politics need characters.
  • Pay attention to Halberstam’s comparison of Iacocca to Ed Koch and Howard Cosell. The point is not that they are identical, but that television fame rewards insecurity, combativeness, and performance.
  • Look for the irony that Iacocca starts resembling the man he resents: socially distant from old colleagues, drawn to celebrity friends, involved in public causes, and playing subordinates against one another.
  • The least vertically integrated Chrysler matters politically. Iacocca can sound like the defender of American industry while benefiting from the freedom to buy from cheaper Asian suppliers.
  • Ford’s handling of archives and documents is a small but revealing mechanism: power protects itself partly by leaving less evidence for history.
  • The chapter’s title is double-edged. “The New American Hero” names Iacocca’s public role, but Halberstam keeps showing the private hunger and cultural machinery underneath it.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**Iacocca with his ghostwriter and editor.**

Setup: He begins by saying he will not talk about Henry Ford because the book already has too much of him. Action: The grievance pours out anyway. Payoff: The memoir is established as less a business autobiography than an unresolved argument with Ford.

**The Curran birthday invitation.**

Setup: Bill Curran’s friends want both Ford and Iacocca at a small birthday gathering. Action: Iacocca signals he will not attend if Ford might be there; Ford declines to spare embarrassment. Payoff: Ford’s restraint makes Iacocca’s obsession look larger and more exposed.

**Najeeb Halaby’s bonus remark to Doug Fraser.**

Setup: Chrysler’s board rewards Iacocca after the company’s first good year. Action: Halaby imagines Henry Ford reading about Lee’s bonus; Fraser thinks instead about union jobs and wonders whether this is all ego. Payoff: The scene compresses the chapter’s tension between public industrial stakes and private vindication.

**The missing Ford papers.**

Setup: Writers researching Ford history find important archival papers missing. Action: Henry Ford half-recognizes that destroying records sounds like something he would do; an aide confirms he had done away with them. Payoff: Ford’s own relationship to history is exposed as evasive, controlled, and anti-documentary.

**The Iacocca book becomes an event.**

Setup: Expectations are modest: a business memoir, a small advance, a ghostwriter without a percentage, and an agent who is basically Iacocca’s barber. Action: Sales explode into millions of copies and dozens of printings. Payoff: Iacocca’s personal feud becomes a national commodity.

**Russell Baker’s warning column.**

Setup: Iacocca is being discussed as a presidential possibility. Action: Baker urges him not to turn from first-class automaker into second-rate president; Iacocca writes back denying he will run. Payoff: The denial only deepens the political theater, because public refusal is read as political courtship.

Cast And Terms

**Lee Iacocca**: Former Ford executive, Chrysler chairman, author, television spokesman, and rising national celebrity. His Chrysler success is shadowed by rage over being fired by Henry Ford II.

**Henry Ford II**: Ford family patriarch and former head of Ford Motor Company. Retired from daily control but still socially and institutionally powerful.

**Edsel Ford II**: Henry Ford II’s son, viewed by Iacocca as the family heir Ford was trying to protect.

**Chrysler**: The rescued automaker whose rebound makes Iacocca famous and gives him a platform beyond Detroit.

**Ford Motor Company / the Glass House**: Ford’s corporate headquarters and institutional world, still shaped by family ownership and hierarchy.

**Doug Fraser**: United Auto Workers leader and Chrysler board member, more focused on jobs and union survival than Iacocca’s personal feud.

**Najeeb Halaby**: Chrysler board member whose remark about Henry Ford seeing Iacocca’s bonus reveals the revenge logic surrounding Lee’s success.

**Bunkie Knudsen**: Former auto executive who understands Ford firings and thinks Iacocca is wasting himself by feuding with Henry Ford.

**Kathy DuRoss Ford**: Henry Ford II’s third wife, part of his later-life social world and a sign of his move away from old Detroit circles.

**Grosse Pointe**: Elite Detroit suburb where auto money, family status, houses, and social exclusion all carry symbolic weight.

**“Stuffing his mouth with dollar bills”**: Detroit phrase for paying a departing executive enough to ensure silence. Iacocca’s public attack breaks that expectation.

**Statue of Liberty campaign**: Public restoration drive that helps turn Iacocca from businessman into national civic symbol.
