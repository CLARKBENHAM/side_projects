Orientation Before Reading

  • Early-1950s Nissan becomes Halberstam’s test case for postwar Japan: a weak industrial management class faces a newly energized labor movement under conditions of hunger, cold, inflation, and ruined legitimacy.
  • The central institutional conflict is not simply company versus union. It is also old Japanese hierarchy versus imported American labor law, company-based loyalty versus industry-wide class organization, and capital investment versus wages.
  • Nissan is unusually vulnerable. Its founder Yoshisuke Ayukawa and other natural leaders were purged by the Americans for wartime ties to Manchuria and the military, leaving management thin, defensive, and uncertain.
  • The Nissan union is unusually strong. Its leader, Tetsuo Masuda, is brilliant, forceful, left-wing, independent of the Communist Party, and trying to make labor power real inside a society still organized around hierarchy.
  • Katsuji Kawamata, “the banker” from the Industrial Bank of Japan, enters as a finance man with no car background but with access to capital, austerity logic, and the hard authority Nissan’s existing managers lack.
  • The larger stakes are national: whether Japan’s auto industry can survive, modernize, and discipline labor; whether radical unions can reshape Japanese capitalism; and whether American occupation policies will ultimately strengthen or weaken business control.

The Chapter Arc

  • Nissan begins as the obvious labor battleground because it is major, Tokyo-based, and poorly led, making it a more attractive target than Toyota’s company-town structure.
  • Halberstam widens the frame from Nissan to postwar Japan, showing why rebellion became plausible: the old order had failed in war, daily life was reduced to food and warmth, and American occupation policy legitimized unions.
  • Nissan’s corporate weakness is traced back to Ayukawa’s rise: his Manchurian, militarist-linked expansion made Nissan powerful before 1945 and morally compromised after defeat.
  • The leadership vacuum brings in Taichi Minoura, who cannot handle finance, technology, or labor, and asks the Industrial Bank of Japan for help.
  • Kawamata arrives from the IBJ expecting to impose financial order, but he finds a company where workers assume bank money means higher wages and management is afraid to say no.
  • The conflict hardens after the Dodge Line, when austerity forces Nissan to cut workers; Kawamata uses the crisis to fire roughly 1,760 employees and wins the resulting forty-day strike.
  • Masuda’s biography explains why the union fight is moral, not just economic, for him: war and bombing have alienated him from the old Japan and from the managers who represent it.
  • Kawamata’s internal power grows as Asahara becomes president in 1951; Asahara has the title, but Kawamata increasingly controls decisions, personnel, money, and the antiunion line.
  • The Korean War temporarily rescues Nissan with American orders, but the boom leaves unresolved the deepest question: should scarce money go to workers’ wages or to machines needed for modernization?
  • By spring 1953, both sides are moving toward a decisive showdown, while Masuda’s friends warn that management, banks, and other industrialists are preparing to crush him.

What To Watch For

  • The American occupation cuts both ways: it purges business leaders and authorizes unions, but later the Dodge Line and anti-Communist policy strengthen austerity and management discipline.
  • Nissan’s weakness is psychological as much as financial. The managers who remain doubt their legitimacy because the company’s prewar success was bound to militarism and Manchuria.
  • The IBJ functions almost like a sovereign power over industry, allocating scarce capital and sending bankers into firms when financial discipline is needed.
  • Kawamata’s lack of technical knowledge matters less than his control of money. Halberstam is showing a version of postwar capitalism in which finance can seize managerial authority.
  • Masuda’s attempt at an industry-wide auto union threatens Japanese custom more than ordinary wage demands do. It would organize workers by class rather than by company.
  • The union’s moral language and management’s investment language do not meet. Masuda sees workers before machines; management sees modernization as the condition for survival.
  • Personal style becomes power. Masuda’s public debating, Kawamata’s bluntness, and Asahara’s evasiveness are not colorful side notes; they shape who can command authority.
  • The Korean War is treated as an external shock that saves Japanese industry without resolving its internal conflicts.
  • Masuda’s great weakness is inflexibility. His confidence, once a source of strength, begins to isolate him from warnings that the balance of power has changed.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**The first postwar Nissan truck**: In early 1946, Robert Alexander watches Nissan produce its first postwar truck in front of the Emperor. The truck has no cab or fenders and then fails to start. The scene compresses the whole industrial problem: national hope exists, but the material base is nearly broken.

**Kawamata arriving in Hiroshima**: Sent away from Tokyo, Kawamata reaches devastated Hiroshima before dawn, carrying army gear and finding the bank half ruined and functioning almost like a soup kitchen. This humiliation helps explain why his later move to Nissan becomes a chance to remake himself.

**Kawamata’s first day at Nissan**: He expects a board meeting and learns the executives are all tied up in wage negotiations. Workers think his arrival means easier loans and larger raises; he thinks it means discipline and an end to labor chaos. The misunderstanding defines the coming fight.

**Masuda baiting managers in the plant**: Masuda walks through the factory, draws managers into public debate, and defeats them before workers who have never seen authority challenged on their behalf. His power comes not only from demands but from making workers feel historically visible.

**The Dodge Line dismissals**: Kawamata secretly raises money for termination pay, announces mass firings, and survives a forty-day strike. The dismissals stand, marking the first major management victory and making Kawamata the real force inside Nissan.

**The Malayan Language Study Group warning**: Hatada gathers Masuda’s old friends to tell him he is overreaching and that management now has allies, loans, and force behind it. Masuda refuses to listen. The scene signals that moral certainty has become strategic blindness.

Cast And Terms

**Katsuji Kawamata**: Banker from the Industrial Bank of Japan sent to Nissan in 1947. Knows finance, not cars; gradually becomes Nissan’s dominant executive and the union’s hardest adversary.

**Tetsuo Masuda**: Nissan union leader, Todai graduate, radicalized by war and postwar deprivation. Charismatic, anti-hierarchy, independent of the Communist Party, and committed to worker control.

**Yoshisuke Ayukawa**: Founder of Nissan and the Nissan-Hitachi combine. Brilliant entrepreneur tied to Manchuria and the military; purged after the war.

**Genshichi Asahara**: Scientist and Ayukawa protégé who returns from purge to become Nissan president. Personally gentle and technically minded, but weak in labor confrontation.

**Shoji Yamamoto**: Able former Nissan leader and possible post-purge presidential candidate; Kawamata blocks him by backing Asahara.

**Taichi Minoura**: Nissan president after Yamamoto’s purge; overwhelmed by labor and finance, asks the IBJ to send help.

**Industrial Bank of Japan / IBJ**: Powerful conservative lender that allocates scarce capital and can effectively determine whether companies live or die.

**MITI**: Ministry of International Trade and Industry, central Japanese bureaucracy guiding industrial policy and capital allocation.

**Dodge Line**: American-imposed austerity policy that squeezed credit, forced layoffs, and strengthened management’s argument for discipline.

**Nikkeiren**: Antiunion business labor-policy organization; represents the wider industrial front against militant unions.

**Todai**: Tokyo University, elite training ground for Japan’s governing class. Masuda’s decision to side with labor is shocking because Todai graduates are expected to join and preserve the hierarchy.

**Zaibatsu**: Large industrial-financial combines, often family-based. Nissan was a newer, more rapid zaibatsu-like formation tied to wartime expansion.

**Korean War boom**: American military demand for trucks, jeeps, repairs, and supplies that suddenly revives Japanese industry, including Nissan and Toyota.

What To Hold Lightly

  • The detailed descriptions of postwar food, cold, and overheated later taxis are vivid atmosphere; keep the deprivation, but do not get lost in every example.
  • Kawamata’s clothes, tie, naps, and coarse manners matter mainly as signs of deliberate power style, not as personality trivia.
  • The artificial sake detail attached to Asahara is memorable, but its function is to show his preference for invention over labor combat.
  • The caricature sketch of “the banker’s car” is useful as evidence of resentment inside management, not as a major event.
  • Masuda’s island origins and school background matter less than the larger fact that he was an elite Todai man who chose labor against hierarchy.
