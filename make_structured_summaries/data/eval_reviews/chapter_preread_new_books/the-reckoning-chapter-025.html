1. Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter begins in 1960, when Yutaka Katayama is sent from Nissan in Tokyo to inspect its tiny California operation. The assignment looks like exile, but it becomes the opening through which Nissan learns America.
  • Katayama is politically vulnerable inside Nissan. His sponsor Kyoichi Harashina has lost influence, his old advertising job is gone, and Nissan’s leadership does not trust his freewheeling style.
  • The American market is both a prize and a mystery. Nissan wants exports and hard currency, but its first Datsuns are badly unsuited to American roads, weather, buyers, and service expectations.
  • The central conflict is not only Nissan versus Detroit or Volkswagen. It is Katayama in America versus Tokyo’s assumptions about what a car should be, what a dealer is worth, and how much foreign-market adaptation is acceptable.
  • California matters because it is less tradition-bound than the East, more open to novelty, and especially receptive to small pickups. Katayama’s “lesser” West Coast assignment turns out to be better ground than the supposedly richer East.
  • Watch the contrast between Katayama and Takashi Ishihara. Katayama understands America from the ground; Ishihara has the internal Nissan authority to make Tokyo listen.

2. The Chapter Arc

  • Katayama’s exile changes into opportunity: what Tokyo treats as a marginal assignment becomes the post that fits his temperament better than any job in Japan.
  • His first encounter with California changes his sense of business possibility. America’s informality, openness to foreigners, and speed of decision give him room he never had inside Nissan’s hierarchy.
  • The Datsun changes from product to problem. Katayama sees that the car is not merely hard to sell; it is mechanically and culturally wrong for American buyers.
  • The U-team engineers become a channel into Tokyo. Katayama turns visiting engineers into witnesses and allies, because engineers can validate problems that Tokyo would dismiss if he reported them alone.
  • The dealer network changes from afterthought to strategy. Katayama realizes that in America dealers are the company’s real customers and builds a hungry, unconventional West Coast network around that fact.
  • The American beachhead shifts from passenger cars to pickups. Nissan wants status through cars, but the small truck, cheap and durable, becomes the product that actually carries the operation.
  • Tokyo’s skepticism changes into conditional support only through Ishihara. His finance background and company standing allow him to push requests that Katayama, perceived as too American, cannot.
  • The U.S. operation moves from near-collapse to survival. Nissan burns through its first million dollars, Ishihara risks his career for more capital, and only in 1964 does the American company finally show a profit.

3. What To Watch For

  • Katayama’s strength is translation: he converts American complaints, habits, climates, freeways, dealers, and service expectations into demands Nissan engineers can understand.
  • Tokyo repeatedly mistakes Japanese use patterns for universal truths. The cold-start argument in New Jersey is the clearest example: what works in Hokkaido fails when Americans treat cars as appliances rather than prized possessions.
  • Dealer economics are causal, not decorative. Katayama pays dealers more because a weak, unknown brand needs dealers who are hungry, loyal, and profitable.
  • The pickup succeeds because it fits California’s mixed identity: work vehicle, commuter vehicle, rural symbol, antistatus object, and inexpensive durable machine.
  • Nissan’s export push depends on Japan’s controlled economy. Domestic taxes and savings discipline help make export pricing possible, even when a Datsun can be cheaper in Los Angeles than Tokyo.
  • The chapter keeps separating engineering willingness from financial reluctance. Engineers may want to improve cars; finance worries about debt, factories, scarce cash, and the risk of America.
  • “Going American” is both accusation and advantage. Katayama’s adoption of American business habits makes him suspect in Tokyo but effective with customers, dealers, and local partners.
  • Volkswagen functions as the model competitor. It proves there is a neglected American market for cheap, durable, well-serviced transportation, especially among buyers Detroit underserves.

4. Load-Bearing Scenes

**Katayama arrives in California.**

Setup: Nissan sends him to America because Tokyo has no comfortable place for him. Action: he discovers a business culture with open collars, quick decisions, and fewer barriers against foreigners. Payoff: exile becomes liberation, and the man least suited to Nissan’s internal politics becomes unusually suited to America.

**“Katayama University.”**

Setup: Tokyo sends small engineering teams to study American requirements. Action: Katayama relentlessly briefs them on overheated interiors, weak performance, and American expectations. Payoff: he creates technical allies who can carry evidence back into the bureaucracy.

**Ray Lemke gets one car.**

Setup: Katayama needs dealers, but famous or wealthy dealers are not lining up to sell unknown Japanese cars. Action: he chooses a hungry mechanic-used-car man in San Diego, gives him one Datsun, and promises another if he sells it. Payoff: the dealer network becomes a bottom-up engine of growth, built from people Detroit would never have chosen.

**The New Jersey cold-start test.**

Setup: Tokyo insists Datsuns start in Hokkaido, so New Jersey complaints must be exaggerated. Action: Usami takes engineers to Greenwood Lake at four in the morning to measure batteries, oil viscosity, and temperature while the engine almost never starts. Payoff: Tokyo’s abstract comparison gives way to observed American reality.

**Ishihara asks for more money.**

Setup: Nissan’s American company spends nearly all its first $1 million far faster than expected. Action: Ishihara goes back to the board twice, finally staking his career on another $500,000. Payoff: America becomes a corporate gamble, not a side experiment, and Nissan survives just long enough to prove the gamble.

5. Cast And Terms

  • **Yutaka Katayama / Mr. K**: Nissan’s West Coast leader in America; energetic, politically suspect in Tokyo, and central to adapting Datsun to American buyers.
  • **Datsun**: Nissan’s American brand name at the time; used for the early cars and pickups sold in the United States.
  • **Nissan**: Japanese automaker trying to establish itself in the U.S. export market.
  • **Kyoichi Harashina**: Katayama’s friend and sponsor, pushed aside in Nissan politics.
  • **Kawamata**: Powerful Nissan leader tied to the conservative, bank-oriented side of management.
  • **Soichi Kawazoe**: Katayama’s East Coast counterpart, better connected in Tokyo and initially given the supposedly better territory.
  • **Takashi Ishihara**: Senior Nissan executive, finance man, later president of the American branch; crucial internal ally for the U.S. effort.
  • **Masataka Usami**: Nissan executive in New Jersey who demonstrates the cold-start problem to Tokyo engineers.
  • **Nobe Wakatsuki**: Trading company executive who had earlier urged Nissan to show cars in Los Angeles.
  • **U teams**: Nissan study teams sent to the United States to learn American technical and market requirements.
  • **Volkswagen**: The dominant import competitor and Katayama’s practical model for service, durability, and appeal to neglected buyers.
  • **Hokkaido**: Cold northern Japanese island used by Tokyo as a misleading comparison for American winter conditions.
  • **Greenwood Lake, New Jersey**: Site of the cold-weather starting tests that prove American conditions and habits differ from Japanese assumptions.

6. What To Hold Lightly

  • Katayama’s delight in American ceremonies, hats, barbecues, fish fries, and dealership openings is revealing, but the business mechanism matters more than the color.
  • The “mobile coffin” nickname captures how bad the early Datsun felt, but the specific defects matter more: heat, weak engine, poor acceleration, weak brakes, and cold-start failure.
  • The Go-Go-Mobile detail helps sketch Ray Lemke’s oddball background; the key point is that Katayama recruited hungry car people who knew poorer customers.
  • Ishihara’s physical presence and rugby background add texture. His real importance is his internal credibility with Nissan finance and the board.
