Orientation Before Reading

  • The chapter centers on Nissan in the late 1970s, especially Takashi Ishihara’s belated rise to the presidency after four decades inside the company.
  • Ishihara is both an old Nissan survivor and a relative outsider to Japan’s elite networks: not from the top schools, not from the ministries, not from the prestige industries.
  • The immediate business crisis is the American market in 1978: small Japanese cars are piling up just as U.S. buyers return to large V-8 cars after treating the first oil shock as temporary.
  • The larger geopolitical hinge is Iran. The fall of the Shah turns what looks like a Japanese inventory disaster into a second oil-price shock and a new opening for small cars.
  • Halberstam also shifts from Nissan’s internal succession to the social rank of the auto industry inside Japan: automobiles are becoming too powerful to ignore, but still lack the old legitimacy of steel, shipping, and the ministries.
  • Katsuji Kawamata, Nissan’s dominant chairman, embodies auto’s rise and its awkwardness: powerful, ambitious, useful, but viewed by older Japanese business elites as crude and arriviste.
  • The conflict is not only Japan versus Detroit. It is also new Japanese industry versus old Japanese industry, especially automobiles versus steel.

The Chapter Arc

  • Ishihara begins as a marginal young employee in a second-tier company, but wartime circumstance and Nissan’s weakness give him room to rise faster than he could have in Japan’s more prestigious institutions.
  • Nissan’s postwar fragility teaches Ishihara the company’s older survival ethic: cash shortages, supplier pressure, bank timing, and improvisation become part of his managerial memory.
  • His career stalls amid Nissan politics, then revives when exports and small cars begin to matter; his successful push for a one-liter domestic car proves he can see a market Kawamata resists.
  • After being passed over in 1973 for a union loyalist, Ishihara finally becomes president in 1977, already sixty-five, inheriting both opportunity and institutional constraint.
  • In 1978, the American market turns against Japanese small cars, forcing Ishihara to prepare for severe production cuts and a company-wide sharing of pain.
  • The Iranian revolution changes the meaning of the crisis: expensive oil threatens all automakers, but threatens large-car producers first, turning Nissan’s small-car base into an advantage.
  • Ishihara moves from planned cutbacks to increased production, while American executives later frame the Japanese recovery as luck; Halberstam lets Ishihara answer that luck had been earned by years of positioning.
  • The chapter then widens to Japan’s industrial hierarchy, showing automobiles displacing older prestige sectors like steel and shipping.
  • Auto’s new leverage over steel becomes a quiet reversal of power: the former supplicant industry can now dictate price terms while preserving public rituals of deference.

What To Watch For

  • Ishihara’s “luck” is not random good fortune alone. Halberstam wants you to see preparation, product mix, and institutional memory as the conditions that let luck matter.
  • Inventory is the chapter’s first hard constraint. Five months of unsold cars in America is not an abstraction; it threatens production schedules, workers’ income, plant communities, and managerial credibility.
  • Oil price changes alter relative advantage. The same small cars that look like stranded assets in late 1978 become strategic assets once gasoline prices rise again.
  • Nissan’s labor system shapes Ishihara’s response. He avoids closing one factory outright and instead plans to spread the burden, protecting communities and distributing sacrifice.
  • The American “year of the Whopper” is not just a colorful Detroit phrase. It marks a mistaken belief that the first oil shock was a passing disturbance rather than a structural warning.
  • Kawamata’s power comes with social liability. He represents an industry too important to exclude but too new and self-assertive to be fully accepted by Japan’s old business establishment.
  • Steel’s decline is relative, not absolute. It remains technically excellent and historically honored, but it loses bargaining power as automobiles become the more dynamic industry.
  • Watch the difference between public ceremony and private power. In the steel-price negotiation, appearances preserve steel’s dignity while auto quietly sets the real limit.
  • Halberstam is tracking hierarchy as much as strategy: who bows, who no longer bows, and who resents the change.

Load-Bearing Scenes

**The Saturday checks at bankrupt Nissan.**

Setup: In 1945, Nissan is so short of cash that suppliers arrive on Saturdays expecting payment before bank loans arrive Monday. Action: Ishihara assigns Yoshihisa Yokoyama to write checks slowly and badly, using mistakes and the noon bank closing to stall payment. Payoff: The scene fixes Nissan’s early postwar desperation in concrete terms and explains Ishihara’s later instinct for survival under pressure.

**The one-liter car fight.**

Setup: In the mid-1960s, Ishihara pushes for a small domestic car while Kawamata strongly opposes it. Action: Ishihara gets the car built and opens a separate sales channel. Payoff: The car becomes Nissan’s best seller, marking Ishihara as someone able to read a market before the company’s dominant elder accepts it.

**The 1978 inventory panic.**

Setup: American buyers return to large cars, and Japanese small cars pile up in California. Action: Ishihara prepares to eliminate export production for months, first cutting overtime and holiday work, then planning reductions spread across all factories. Payoff: The crisis shows how quickly export success can reverse and how production decisions in Japan are tied to American taste.

**Iran turns the crisis inside out.**

Setup: Ishihara initially cannot believe the Shah might fall and does not immediately grasp the business implications. Action: As oil jumps from $16 to $20 a barrel and keeps rising, he realizes the threat is comparative: big cars will be hit first. Payoff: Nissan’s planned contraction becomes expansion, and the Japanese small-car makers regain the advantage.

**Auto versus steel over prices.**

Setup: Steel, once dominant and government-backed, wants higher prices from the automakers. Action: Auto executives privately decide the acceptable increase, communicate it quietly, and let steel publicly appear to fight for that exact number. Payoff: The scene reveals auto’s new authority: it no longer needs to humiliate steel publicly because it already controls the outcome.

Cast And Terms

**Takashi Ishihara**: Nissan executive who joined in 1937 and became president in 1977; a survivor of Nissan’s lean years and a key advocate of small cars.

**Katsuji Kawamata**: Powerful Nissan chairman; politically shrewd, close to the union, and viewed by old-line business leaders as an upstart.

**Yoshihisa Yokoyama**: Young Nissan employee assigned to write supplier checks slowly during the company’s postwar cash crisis.

**Tadahiro Iwakoshi**: Union loyalist chosen over Ishihara for Nissan’s presidency in 1973.

**Keidanren**: Japan’s powerful federation of business organizations; a marker of elite industrial status and influence.

**MITI**: Ministry of International Trade and Industry, central to Japan’s postwar industrial policy.

**Ministry of Finance**: Another elite ministry whose former officials often moved into favored industries.

**Nippon Kokan**: Major Japanese steel company; appears in the chapter as part of steel’s declining leverage against auto.

**Yom Kippur War / first oil shock**: The 1973 war that triggered a huge rise in oil prices and initially helped Japanese small-car makers.

**Iranian Revolution / fall of the Shah**: The 1978-79 upheaval that produces a second oil shock and renews demand for fuel-efficient cars.

**Arrivistes**: Upstarts; here, the old establishment’s view of auto executives newly entering Japan’s highest business ranks.
